\section{Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:eval_setup}

\paragraph{Benchmark.}
All models are evaluated on NanoBEIR~\citep{nanobeir2024}, a
downsampled variant of the Benchmarking Information Retrieval (BEIR)
benchmark~\citep{thakur2021beir} in which each corpus is capped at a few
thousand passages to enable rapid comparison of large numbers of model
variants.  Thirteen task subsets are included, spanning open-domain
question answering, fact verification, entity search, argument retrieval,
and scientific information retrieval [Table~5.1].  Each subset provides
50 test queries (49 for NanoTouche2020) and a corpus of 1,866--6,045
passages, yielding 649 total queries and 56,352 total corpus passages
across all subsets.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{NanoBEIR subsets used for evaluation.}
  \label{tab:nanobeir_subsets}
  [Table 5.1 placeholder: subset name | domain/task type | \# queries |
  corpus size]
\end{table}

\paragraph{Encoder backbones.}
Three 768-dimensional sentence encoders serve as backbones: (i)
BAAI/bge-base-en-v1.5 (BGE), a retrieval-specialised model; (ii)
roberta-base (RoBERTa), a general-purpose masked language model; and
(iii) all-mpnet-base-v2 (MPNet), a general-purpose sentence encoder.
Each backbone is evaluated in two regimes: the publicly released
pre-trained checkpoint and a retrieval fine-tuned version trained with
Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (MNRL) over eight mixed retrieval
datasets, as described in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.  Because all
three backbones produce 768-dimensional float32 embeddings in their
default configuration, they share a common reference point for
cross-method and cross-backbone comparison.

\paragraph{Compression methods.}
Compression configurations are drawn from three families, each
introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}:
\emph{post-hoc precision reduction} (INT8, binary quantisation, Product
Quantisation (PQ), and TurboQuant (TQ));
\emph{training-time binarisation} (the Straight-Through Estimator
binarisation layer (BAT) and annealed tanh binarisation (atanh) at three
annealing rates $\gamma \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.20\}$); and
\emph{training-time dimensionality reduction} via Matryoshka
Representation Learning (MRL), which produces truncated float32
embeddings at five sub-dimensions (32, 64, 128, 256, 512).
Selected MRL checkpoints are further combined with binary quantisation,
TQ, and PQ to yield stacked compression.

\paragraph{Compression ratio.}
The compression ratio $r$ is defined relative to the float32 baseline:
a 768-dimensional float32 embedding occupies $768 \times 4 = 3{,}072$
bytes.  For scalar precision reduction to $b$ bits per component,
$r = 32 / b$ (e.g.\ INT8: $4{\times}$; binary: $32{\times}$).  For MRL
truncation to $d'$ dimensions, $r = 768 / d'$.  For PQ with $M$
subspaces and $n$-bit codes per subspace,
$r = (768 \times 32) / (M \times n)$.
Combined methods multiply the per-axis ratios; for example, MRL-256
followed by binary quantisation gives $(768/256) \times 32 = 96{\times}$.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
The primary metric is Mean Reciprocal Rank at depth 10 (MRR@10),
macro-averaged equally over all 13 subsets.  MRR@10 reflects the rank
of the first relevant document in the top-10 retrieved results and is
well-suited to short-list retrieval settings.  Secondary metrics,
Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain at 10 (NDCG@10) and Recall at $k$
for $k \in \{1,3,5,10\}$, are computed for all configurations but
serve only as supplementary analysis.

\paragraph{Similarity functions.}
Float32 and INT8 embeddings are scored by cosine similarity.  Binary
embeddings (one bit per original dimension, packed as uint8) are scored
by Hamming distance, which is equivalent to inner-product similarity
over $\{-1,+1\}$ codes and admits fast bitwise XOR-popcount computation.
PQ and TQ retrieval use asymmetric distance estimation: the query
embedding is decoded to float32 before scoring while corpus embeddings
remain in their compressed form.

\paragraph{Statistical significance.}
Pairwise significance tests are conducted for every model pair on each
NanoBEIR subset using a paired permutation test applied to per-query MRR
scores at significance level $\alpha = 0.05$.  Aggregated pairwise
significance patterns are visualised as a heat map in [Figure~5.X].

\paragraph{Effect of fine-tuning.}
The three backbones exhibit qualitatively different responses to
retrieval fine-tuning.  RoBERTa-base and MPNet-base enter with
near-chance MRR@10 scores of 0.097 and 0.156 respectively, consistent
with their pre-training objectives (masked language modelling and
general-purpose sentence similarity) providing no direct signal for
asymmetric passage retrieval.  Fine-tuning with Multiple Negatives
Ranking Loss (MNRL) over eight mixed retrieval datasets yields gains of
43.4 and 42.6 percentage points, lifting both models to competitive
retrieval performance (0.530 and 0.582).  This confirms that the
contrastive fine-tuning procedure is effective at reshaping
general-purpose representations into retrieval-oriented embedding spaces
regardless of the pre-training objective.

BGE-base-en-v1.5 (BGE) behaves differently: fine-tuning produces a
small but consistent \emph{decrease} of 0.6 percentage points
(0.691~$\to$~0.685).  BGE has already undergone large-scale retrieval
pre-training; further adaptation on the comparatively small mixed-domain
corpus used here offers no additional benefit and may introduce minor
distribution mismatch.  This has a direct consequence for compression
evaluation: for BGE, the fine-tuned float32 ceiling is slightly
\emph{below} the pre-trained ceiling, so apparent recovery toward 0.691
under compression should be interpreted with care.  Unless stated
otherwise, all compression results in subsequent sections are reported
relative to the fine-tuned float32 for each backbone.

\paragraph{Cross-backbone gap.}
Even after fine-tuning, a substantial absolute gap separates BGE from
the two general-purpose backbones: BGE exceeds fine-tuned MPNet by 6.6
percentage points and fine-tuned RoBERTa by 15.5 percentage points.
This gap reflects differences in backbone capacity and the depth of
upstream retrieval pre-training rather than the fine-tuning procedure
itself.  The spread motivates the cross-backbone generalisability
analysis in Section~\ref{sec:cross_backbone}: whether compression
methods preserve rank ordering across backbones or interact differently
with each embedding geometry is examined there.

\section{Post-Hoc Compression}
\label{sec:posthoc_compression}

This section evaluates compression applied after training to the
fine-tuned float32 checkpoints, with no modification to model weights.
Four methods are examined in increasing order of compression depth:
INT8 scalar quantisation (Section~\ref{subsec:int8}), post-hoc binary
quantisation (Section~\ref{subsec:binary}), Product Quantisation
(Section~\ref{subsec:pq}), and TurboQuant
(Section~\ref{subsec:tq}).  Section~\ref{subsec:cross_method} places
all four methods on a common axis at 32$\times$ compression.

%-------------------------------------------
\subsection{INT8 Scalar Quantisation}
\label{subsec:int8}

INT8 quantisation rounds each embedding component from float32 to a
signed 8-bit integer, reducing per-vector storage from 3{,}072 bytes
to 768 bytes (4$\times$ compression), with similarity still computed
by cosine distance in float after dequantisation.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Mean MRR@10 for INT8 quantisation (4$\times$) compared with
           the fine-tuned float32 baseline.}
  \label{tab:int8}
  [Table 5.X placeholder: Configuration | BGE | RoBERTa | MPNet]
\end{table}

Across all three backbones, INT8 quantisation is essentially lossless
(Table~\ref{tab:int8}).  BGE incurs a loss of 0.7 percentage points
(0.685~$\to$~0.678) and MPNet 1.5 percentage points
(0.582~$\to$~0.567), while RoBERTa shows a marginal \emph{gain} of
0.2 percentage points (0.530~$\to$~0.532).  The RoBERTa gain is
within noise but consistent with INT8 rounding acting as a mild
regulariser on embeddings that were not originally trained for
retrieval.  These results confirm that 4$\times$ precision reduction
via INT8 incurs negligible quality cost for all backbones, making it
the recommended default for memory-constrained deployments where
float32 precision is not required.

%-------------------------------------------
\subsection{Binary Quantisation}
\label{subsec:binary}

Post-hoc binary quantisation thresholds each component at zero,
producing one-bit-per-dimension embeddings packed as uint8 vectors
(96 bytes per vector; 32$\times$ compression).  Retrieval is
performed via Hamming distance.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Mean MRR@10 for post-hoc binary quantisation (32$\times$)
           compared with the fine-tuned float32 baseline.}
  \label{tab:binary}
  [Table 5.X placeholder: Configuration | BGE | RoBERTa | MPNet]
\end{table}

At 32$\times$ compression, post-hoc binary quantisation inflicts
meaningful quality degradation across all backbones
(Table~\ref{tab:binary}): BGE drops 3.9 percentage points
(0.685~$\to$~0.646), MPNet drops 4.8 percentage points
(0.582~$\to$~0.534), and RoBERTa drops 2.9 percentage points
(0.530~$\to$~0.501).  The degradation is expected: collapsing 32
bits to a single sign bit with no adaptation of the embedding geometry
discards the magnitude information that encodes fine-grained relevance
distinctions.  Section~\ref{sec:training_compression} shows that
training-time binarisation methods substantially recover this loss by
learning representations whose sign more faithfully encodes retrieval
signal.

%-------------------------------------------
\subsection{Product Quantisation}
\label{subsec:pq}

Product Quantisation (PQ) partitions the embedding space into $M$
disjoint subspaces and independently quantises each with an $n$-bit
codebook learned from the corpus.  Two configurations are evaluated,
both at 32$\times$ compression: $M{=}96$ subspaces with 8-bit codes
(PQ-$m$96-$n$8) and $M{=}192$ subspaces with 4-bit codes
(PQ-$m$192-$n$4).  Retrieval uses asymmetric distance estimation,
keeping only the query vector in float32 at query time.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Mean MRR@10 for PQ configurations (32$\times$) compared with
           the fine-tuned float32 baseline and post-hoc binary.}
  \label{tab:pq}
  [Table 5.X placeholder: Configuration | BGE | RoBERTa | MPNet]
\end{table}

PQ substantially outperforms post-hoc binary quantisation at the same
32$\times$ ratio (Table~\ref{tab:pq}).  For BGE, PQ-$m$96-$n$8
reaches 0.679, recovering over 80\,\% of the gap between post-hoc
binary and the float32 baseline (3.3 percentage points recovered of
3.9).  For MPNet the gain over post-hoc binary is 2.8 percentage
points (0.534~$\to$~0.562).  RoBERTa benefits less: both
configurations score within 0.001 of each other (0.514 and 0.519),
suggesting the RoBERTa embedding geometry is less amenable to the
Voronoi-cell structure assumed by PQ after fine-tuning.

Between the two PQ configurations, PQ-$m$96-$n$8 is preferable for
BGE and MPNet (0.679 vs 0.675 and 0.562 vs 0.549), while
PQ-$m$192-$n$4 has a marginal edge for RoBERTa (0.519 vs 0.514).
The 8-bit-per-subspace variant benefits from finer codebook resolution
at the cost of a larger number of centroids per subspace.

%-------------------------------------------
\subsection{TurboQuant}
\label{subsec:tq}

TurboQuant (TQ) is a corpus-fitted quantiser that rotates embeddings
into a whitened basis before applying uniform scalar quantisation at
$b$ bits per component.  Three bit-widths are evaluated:
1-bit (32$\times$), 2-bit (16$\times$), and 4-bit (8$\times$).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  [Figure 5.X placeholder: TQ quality--compression curve for all three
  backbones, with float32 baseline marked as a horizontal dashed line.]
  \caption{Mean MRR@10 vs compression ratio for TurboQuant across
           bit-widths and backbones.}
  \label{fig:tq_curve}
\end{figure}

At 4-bit precision (8$\times$ compression), TQ is virtually lossless
for all backbones (Figure~\ref{fig:tq_curve}).  BGE scores 0.685,
matching the fine-tuned float32 ceiling to within 0.03 percentage
points; RoBERTa marginally exceeds its float32 baseline
(0.532~$>$~0.530); and MPNet scores 0.579 against a float32
ceiling of 0.582 ($-$0.3 percentage points).  This near-lossless
behaviour at 8$\times$ makes TQ-4bit the best post-hoc method by
quality-per-byte up to that compression level.

At 2-bit precision (16$\times$), TQ achieves 0.678, 0.530, and 0.574
for BGE, RoBERTa, and MPNet respectively.  For BGE, TQ-2bit
(16$\times$, 192 bytes) is Pareto-dominated by PQ-$m$96-$n$8
(32$\times$, 96 bytes): PQ achieves higher MRR@10 (0.679 vs 0.678)
at half the memory cost.  This relationship does not hold for
RoBERTa and MPNet, where TQ-2bit retains a quality advantage over
PQ at any compression ratio (0.530 vs 0.514 and 0.574 vs 0.562).

At 1-bit precision (32$\times$), TQ (0.666, 0.519, 0.547) outperforms
post-hoc binary quantisation (0.646, 0.501, 0.534) by 2.1, 1.7, and
1.4 percentage points for BGE, RoBERTa, and MPNet respectively.  The
whitening rotation redistributes variance more uniformly across
dimensions before thresholding, preserving more retrieval signal per
bit than a raw sign quantisation.

Section~\ref{sec:training_compression}, provide a complementary path
to closing this remaining gap without relying on corpus-side fitting.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Training-Time Compression}
\label{sec:training_compression}

Unlike post-hoc methods, training-time compression modifies the
fine-tuning procedure itself so that the model learns representations
optimised for the compressed form.  Two families are evaluated:
binarisation-aware training (Section~\ref{subsec:ste_atanh}), which
targets the same 32$\times$ compression ratio as post-hoc binary
quantisation, and Matryoshka Representation Learning
(Section~\ref{subsec:mrl}), which targets dimensionality reduction.

%-------------------------------------------
\subsection{Binarisation-Aware Training: STE and Annealed Tanh}
\label{subsec:ste_atanh}

Two binarisation-aware training methods are evaluated.  The
Straight-Through Estimator binarisation layer (BAT) inserts a
$\mathrm{sign}(x)$ operation into the forward pass and passes
gradients through unchanged; the embedding geometry is thus shaped
by the retrieval loss throughout training.  The annealed tanh
method (atanh) instead uses $\tanh(\beta \cdot x)$ during training,
where $\beta$ grows from 1 to $\approx$28 over the course of
fine-tuning, progressively tightening the approximation to
$\mathrm{sign}(x)$; three annealing rates $\gamma \in
\{0.05, 0.10, 0.20\}$ are evaluated, with higher $\gamma$ producing
faster annealing.  Both methods produce 768-dimensional binary
embeddings at inference (32$\times$ compression, 96 bytes per vector).

           training methods, post-hoc binary, and the float32 baseline.}

\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:ste_atanh} and Figure~\ref{fig:ste_atanh} show that
binarisation-aware training consistently and substantially outperforms
post-hoc binary quantisation at 32$\times$ for all backbones.  BAT
gains 1.3, 6.4, and 4.8 percentage points over post-hoc binary for
BGE, RoBERTa, and MPNet respectively.  The improvement is largest for
the general-purpose backbones, confirming that these models benefit
more from having their embedding geometry explicitly shaped for
binary retrieval.  For MPNet, BAT reaches 0.582, matching the
fine-tuned float32 baseline to within 0.01 percentage points,
a near-lossless result at 32$\times$ compression.  For RoBERTa, BAT
reaches 0.565, exceeding the float32 baseline by 3.5 percentage
points, consistent with the view that contrastive fine-tuning with a
binarisation constraint can discover a more retrieval-effective
geometry than unconstrained float32 training.

Annealed tanh at $\gamma{=}0.2$ follows a similar pattern but with a
backbone-dependent trade-off relative to BAT.  For RoBERTa, atanh
($\gamma{=}0.2$) reaches 0.580, a further 1.5 percentage points above
BAT and 5.0 percentage points above the float32 baseline,
the strongest binary result for RoBERTa across all methods.  The gradual
annealing schedule appears to give the RoBERTa geometry more time to
reorganise around binary boundaries before the binarisation constraint
fully activates.  For BGE and MPNet, however, BAT outperforms atanh
($\gamma{=}0.2$) by 1.0 and 0.8 percentage points respectively (0.658
vs 0.648 and 0.582 vs 0.573), suggesting that the harder straight-through
gradient signal is more effective for backbones that begin with a
stronger retrieval prior.  The results for $\gamma \in \{0.05, 0.10\}$
(Table~\ref{tab:ste_atanh}) show a monotonic relationship between
annealing rate and RoBERTa performance, with slower annealing
consistently under-performing $\gamma{=}0.2$.

\subsection{Matryoshka Representation Learning}
\label{subsec:mrl}

Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) replaces the standard ranking
loss with a weighted sum of losses computed at five nested
sub-dimensions $d \in \{32, 64, 128, 256, 512\}$.  The resulting
model produces a single 768-dimensional float32 embedding whose leading
$d$ dimensions are independently informative, enabling truncation to
any trained sub-dimension at deployment time without retraining.

           for all three backbones.  The float32 768-dim baseline is
           shown as a dashed reference line for each backbone.}
  \label{fig:mrl_curve}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[h]

           with compression ratio and float32 baseline shown for
           reference.}

\end{table}

Figure~\ref{fig:mrl_curve} and Table~\ref{tab:mrl} show that MRL
achieves remarkably graceful quality degradation as $d$ decreases.
At 512 dimensions (1.5$\times$ compression), MRL matches or marginally
exceeds the full 768-dimensional float32 baseline for BGE and
RoBERTa: BGE reaches 0.687 against a float32 ceiling of 0.685
($+$0.2 percentage points), and RoBERTa reaches 0.533 vs 0.530
($+$0.3 percentage points).  MPNet scores 0.579, within 0.3 percentage
points of float32 (0.582).  The slight gains for BGE and RoBERTa
reflect MRL's training incentive to concentrate retrieval signal
into early dimensions: the 512-dimensional prefix may discard
low-variance tail dimensions that contribute noise rather than signal
in the full 768-dimensional space.

At 256 dimensions (3$\times$ compression), quality remains within
1.0 percentage points of float32 for all backbones: BGE scores 0.675
($-$1.0 pp), RoBERTa 0.526 ($-$0.4 pp), and MPNet 0.577 ($-$0.5 pp).
The curve in Figure~\ref{fig:mrl_curve} shows that degradation
accelerates as $d$ falls below 128, where the information bottleneck
becomes more severe.  The primary value of MRL truncation, however,
lies not in stand-alone compression but in enabling stacked
combinations with binary quantisation, TQ, and PQ, which are examined
in Section~\ref{sec:cross_axis}.

\section{Stacked Combinations}
\label{sec:cross_axis}

Stacked combinations pair dimensional reduction (MRL truncation)
with precision reduction to reach compression ratios beyond what either
method achieves alone.  Four pairings are evaluated: MRL+post-hoc binary
and MRL+BAT at 48$\times$, and MRL+TQ and MRL+PQ across a range of
memory budgets.

\begin{table}[h]
  \centering
  \caption{Mean MRR@10 for stacked combinations, with compression
           ratio and memory footprint per vector.  MRL float32 baselines
           are included for reference.}
  \label{tab:cross_axis}
  [Table 5.X placeholder: Method | $r$ | Bytes | BGE | RoBERTa | MPNet]
\end{table}

\paragraph{MRL+post-hoc binary versus MRL+BAT.}
Both MRL-512+post-hoc binary and MRL-512+BAT produce 512-bit binary
vectors (64 bytes; 48$\times$ compression), but differ in whether
binarisation is applied post-hoc to the MRL float32 checkpoint or
jointly trained from scratch.  In the standalone setting
(Section~\ref{subsec:ste_atanh}), BAT substantially outperforms
post-hoc binary for all backbones.  The stacked results in
Table~\ref{tab:cross_axis} reveal a striking reversal: for BGE and
MPNet, post-hoc binary applied to MRL-512 embeddings \emph{outperforms}
jointly trained MRL+BAT by 4.0 and 1.9 percentage points respectively
(BGE: 0.633 vs 0.593; MPNet: 0.540 vs 0.521).  For RoBERTa the
advantage of BAT is preserved but sharply reduced to 1.6 percentage
points (0.517 vs 0.501), compared with the 6.4 percentage point gap
in the standalone setting.

This reversal suggests a significant optimisation conflict in joint
MRL+BAT training.  MRL's loss requires the leading sub-dimensions to
be informative as float32 representations across five nested budgets,
while the straight-through binarisation gradient simultaneously
pushes the geometry toward sign-separable boundaries.  These
constraints appear to work against each other for BGE and MPNet, whose
retrieval-specialised pre-training already anchors the embedding space
strongly.  The two-stage approach (first train MRL for float32
truncation quality, then apply post-hoc binary) avoids this conflict
by optimising each objective independently.  RoBERTa is the exception:
its weaker retrieval prior gives joint training more latitude to
reorganise the embedding geometry, preserving the BAT advantage.

\paragraph{MRL+TQ versus MRL+PQ.}
MRL truncation compounds favourably with both TQ and PQ, enabling
compression ratios in the 32$\times$--96$\times$ range while retaining
meaningful retrieval quality.  Table~\ref{tab:cross_axis} and
Figure~\ref{fig:mrl_cross_axis} report results for the best-performing
configurations at each memory budget.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  [Figure 5.X placeholder: MRL+TQ and MRL+PQ quality--compression
  curves for all three backbones, with standalone TQ and PQ curves
  shown as dashed references.]
  \caption{Mean MRR@10 vs compression ratio for MRL+TQ and MRL+PQ
           stacked combinations.  Standalone TQ and PQ results
           are shown as dashed reference curves.}
  \label{fig:mrl_cross_axis}
\end{figure}

For BGE, MRL+PQ-$m$64-$n$8 reaches 0.673 at 48$\times$ (64 bytes),
exceeding MRL-256+TQ-3bit (0.666 at 32$\times$, 96 bytes) by
0.7 percentage points at \emph{higher} compression.  MRL+PQ therefore
Pareto-dominates MRL+TQ on the BGE quality--compression frontier in
this range: it achieves better quality with less memory.  For RoBERTa
and MPNet this relationship is reversed: MRL+TQ-3bit scores 0.523
and 0.576 against MRL+PQ-$m$64-$n$8's 0.515 and 0.565, so TQ retains
a quality advantage when more memory is available, consistent with
the backbone-dependent behaviour observed in
Section~\ref{subsec:cross_method}.

The MRL+TQ combination also yields the best results at extreme
compression for BGE.  Taking the best-performing MRL+TQ configuration
at each memory budget, the quality envelope for BGE is:
0.373 at 8 bytes (384$\times$), 0.614 at 32 bytes (96$\times$), and
0.666 at 96 bytes (32$\times$).  The 32-byte operating point is
particularly notable: at 96$\times$ compression, BGE retains 0.614
MRR@10, recovering substantial retrieval capability at a memory cost
of just over 2\,\% of the float32 baseline.  The full stacked
quality--compression curves are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mrl_cross_axis}
and discussed further in the Pareto analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:pareto}.

\section{Pareto Analysis}
\label{sec:pareto}

A configuration is Pareto-optimal if no other configuration achieves
both higher retrieval quality \emph{and} higher compression ratio.
The Pareto frontier therefore identifies the set of non-dominated
operating points from which a practitioner should choose, given their
memory budget.  This section characterises the frontier for each
backbone (Section~\ref{subsec:pareto_frontiers}), identifies the best
available method at key compression budgets
(Section~\ref{subsec:best_at_budget}), and analyses which compression
families, post-hoc versus training-time, appear on the frontier and
why (Section~\ref{subsec:train_vs_posthoc}).

Three structural features of the BGE frontier deserve attention.
First, the pre-trained checkpoint (0.691) appears at $1\times$ because
fine-tuning slightly \emph{reduces} BGE quality
(Section~\ref{sec:float32_baselines}); the fine-tuned float32 is thus
dominated by its own pre-trained counterpart at the same memory cost.
Second, TQ occupies the moderate-compression range (8$\times$--10.7$\times$)
with remarkably small quality loss: the step from 1.5$\times$ to
8$\times$ costs only 0.2 percentage points.  Third, PQ takes over
from 32$\times$ onwards: at this ratio, PQ-$m$96-$n$8 (0.679)
outperforms every other method including TQ-1bit (0.666) and BAT
(0.658), both of which are off the frontier at 32$\times$.  Beyond
32$\times$, stacked MRL+PQ combinations extend the frontier
monotonically, with each step compounding both compression dimensions.

The RoBERTa and MPNet frontiers differ from BGE in one important
respect: training-time binarisation methods appear as Pareto-optimal
points at 32$\times$.  For RoBERTa, annealed tanh ($\gamma{=}0.2$)
achieves 0.580 at 32$\times$, exceeding the best post-hoc method at
that ratio (PQ-$m$192-$n$4: 0.519) by 6.1 percentage points; for
MPNet, BAT achieves 0.582, exceeding the best post-hoc method
(PQ-$m$96-$n$8: 0.562) by 2.0 percentage points.  These gaps are large
enough that no post-hoc configuration at any compression ratio
dominates the training-time methods for these backbones, making them
unambiguously Pareto-optimal at 32$\times$.

\subsection{Training-Time versus Post-Hoc Methods on the Frontier}
\label{subsec:train_vs_posthoc}

The Pareto analysis reveals a clear division of labour between the two
compression paradigms.  For BGE, the frontier is composed entirely of
post-hoc methods (TQ, PQ) and stacked combinations built on the
MRL float32 checkpoint; no training-time binarisation method (BAT or
annealed tanh) appears as a Pareto-optimal point.  At 32$\times$, the
canonical comparison ratio for binary retrieval, PQ-$m$96-$n$8 (0.679)
dominates BAT (0.658) and annealed tanh (0.648), meaning that fitting
a product codebook to the corpus is more effective than shaping the
embedding geometry during training for a backbone that already carries
a strong retrieval prior.

For RoBERTa and MPNet, the picture is reversed at 32$\times$:
training-time methods are firmly on the frontier and post-hoc methods
cannot reach them.  The gap of 6.1 percentage points between annealed
tanh and the best post-hoc method for RoBERTa is larger than the total
quality loss from post-hoc binary alone (2.9 pp), underscoring how
profoundly binarisation-aware training reshapes the embedding geometry
when the backbone lacks a retrieval prior.

This backbone-conditioned split has a practical implication: the choice
between training-time and post-hoc compression should be informed by
the retrieval specialisation of the backbone.  For retrieval-specialised
models such as BGE, post-hoc corpus-fitted quantisation achieves
superior results without requiring any retraining.  For general-purpose
models such as RoBERTa and MPNet, investment in binarisation-aware
fine-tuning is necessary to reach the Pareto frontier at high
compression ratios.  No single strategy dominates across all
backbones, reinforcing the need for the controlled cross-backbone
comparison conducted in this thesis.

\section{Cross-Backbone Generalisability}
\label{sec:cross_backbone}

The three backbones span a wide range of retrieval specialisation,
from the retrieval-pretrained BGE to the general-purpose RoBERTa and
MPNet.  This section examines whether compression methods behave
consistently across that range: whether rank orderings are stable,
whether the magnitude of compression-induced quality loss is
backbone-invariant, and whether conclusions drawn on one backbone
transfer to others.

  \centering

\paragraph{Low-compression regime (up to 8$\times$).}
INT8 (4$\times$) and TQ-4bit (8$\times$) are the most backbone-agnostic
methods evaluated.  INT8 incurs losses of 0.7, $-$0.2 (gain), and
1.5 percentage points for BGE, RoBERTa, and MPNet respectively;
TQ-4bit incurs losses of 0.03, $-$0.2 (gain), and 0.3 percentage points.
In both cases the direction of effect is consistent (near-zero) and
the quality ordering of backbones is perfectly preserved:
BGE $>$ MPNet $>$ RoBERTa throughout.  MRL truncation to 512
dimensions (1.5$\times$) similarly shows uniform behaviour: all three
backbones match or marginally exceed their float32 baselines (BGE
$+$0.2 pp, RoBERTa $+$0.3 pp, MPNet $-$0.3 pp).  At 256 dimensions
(3$\times$), losses remain small and consistent (BGE $-$1.0 pp,
RoBERTa $-$0.4 pp, MPNet $-$0.5 pp).  These results indicate that
for compression up to 8$\times$, and for MRL truncation up to 3$\times$,
the choice of backbone does not materially affect the choice of
compression method: all methods are near-lossless and all rank
orderings are stable.

\paragraph{High-compression regime (32$\times$).}
At 32$\times$, backbone identity becomes the dominant factor in method
selection.  Post-hoc binary quantisation degrades all backbones by
2.9--4.8 percentage points in absolute terms, and its rank ordering
relative to float32 is stable (BGE retains 94\,\%, RoBERTa 94\,\%,
MPNet 92\,\%).  However, the relative effectiveness of other methods
diverges sharply.

For post-hoc learned quantisation (PQ), BGE benefits most: PQ retains
99\,\% of its float32 quality, whereas RoBERTa and MPNet retain 97\,\%
and 97\,\% respectively, a smaller but still meaningful advantage over
raw binary.  For training-time binarisation, the effect is strongly
backbone-dependent in direction, not just magnitude: BAT reduces BGE
quality by 2.7 percentage points below float32 but \emph{raises}
RoBERTa quality by 3.5 percentage points and leaves MPNet essentially
unchanged ($-$0.01 pp).  Annealed tanh ($\gamma{=}0.2$) amplifies this
pattern further, raising RoBERTa by 5.0 percentage points above float32
while reducing BGE by 3.7 percentage points.  These opposing effects
mean that a practitioner benchmarking on BGE would conclude that
training-time binarisation underperforms post-hoc PQ, a conclusion
that is the reverse of the truth for RoBERTa.

\paragraph{Rank-ordering stability.}
At low compression, method rankings are fully consistent across
backbones.  At 32$\times$, they are not.  The most consequential
reversal is between training-time and post-hoc methods: for BGE,
PQ $>$ BAT $>$ post-hoc binary; for RoBERTa and MPNet, BAT and atanh
$>$ PQ $>$ post-hoc binary.  Within post-hoc methods, however, the
ordering is more stable: PQ consistently outperforms TQ-1bit which
consistently outperforms post-hoc binary across all backbones, with
only minor variation in the margin.  This suggests that post-hoc
method rankings are transferable across backbones, while conclusions
about training-time versus post-hoc compression are not.

\paragraph{Backbone-independent observations.}
Several findings hold uniformly across all three backbones and can be
stated without qualification.  First, INT8 and TQ-4bit are effectively
lossless at 4$\times$ and 8$\times$ respectively.  Second, post-hoc
binary is always the weakest method at 32$\times$, and learned
quantisation (PQ or training-time) always outperforms it.  Third, MRL
truncation is the most backbone-consistent compression method at every
truncation level, with quality losses that are small, monotonically
increasing with compression ratio, and nearly uniform across backbones.
Fourth, all stacked MRL+precision combinations degrade more
gracefully for BGE than for RoBERTa and MPNet, reflecting the stronger
retrieval geometry of the retrieval-pretrained backbone.

These observations together suggest that evaluation on a single
retrieval-specialised backbone, as is common in prior work, is
sufficient to characterise post-hoc methods but insufficient to
characterise training-time binarisation, whose benefit is
disproportionately concentrated on general-purpose backbones.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Embedding geometry as the central explanatory variable.}
The most consistent explanatory factor across results is the
\emph{retrieval geometry} of the backbone embedding space: the degree
to which directions in the float32 space already encode retrieval
relevance in a form amenable to compression.  BGE, which has undergone
large-scale retrieval pre-training, enters the compression experiments
with a geometry that is already near-optimal for dense retrieval; the
sign of each dimension carries substantial relevance signal, and
corpus-fitted codebooks (PQ) can exploit this structure without
retraining.  RoBERTa and MPNet, whose float32 spaces are shaped by
masked language modelling and general sentence similarity objectives,
do not share this property.  Their signs are not retrieval-informative
until the model is explicitly trained to make them so.

This geometry hypothesis explains several otherwise puzzling results.
It explains why binarisation-aware fine-tuning raises RoBERTa and MPNet
\emph{above} their float32 baselines at 32$\times$: the binary
constraint forces the model to discover a retrieval-oriented geometry
that float32 training, without the constraint, never finds.  It
explains why PQ, which adapts to the existing geometry without changing
it, performs comparably to training-time binarisation for BGE but falls
well short for RoBERTa and MPNet.  And it explains why fine-tuning
slightly \emph{hurts} BGE: the backbone's pre-trained geometry is
already well-adapted for retrieval, and further adaptation on a smaller
mixed-domain corpus provides no benefit and may introduce minor
distributional noise.

\paragraph{Post-hoc corpus fitting as a substitute for geometry
adaptation.}
A secondary theme is the value of corpus-fitted quantisation (PQ and
TQ) as a practical alternative to training-time methods.  Both PQ and
TQ learn a non-linear mapping from the float32 embedding space to a
compact code using only unlabelled corpus embeddings, with no
label information or retraining.  For BGE, this approach reaches the
Pareto frontier at every compression ratio tested, demonstrating that
when the underlying geometry is sound, corpus-side adaptation is
sufficient.  The Pareto dominance of PQ-$m$96-$n$8 over TQ-2bit for
BGE (where PQ achieves higher quality at twice the compression ratio)
illustrates that structured codebook learning extracts substantially
more information from a good geometry than scalar rotation-and-threshold
operations.  The practical implication is that practitioners deploying
retrieval-specialised backbones can avoid the cost and complexity of
retraining by investing instead in corpus-calibrated quantisation.

\paragraph{Limits of joint optimisation in stacked compression.}
The MRL+BAT reversal (Section~\ref{sec:cross_axis}) reveals a
non-obvious failure mode of joint multi-objective training: when two
compression objectives impose conflicting geometric constraints, joint
optimisation can produce embeddings that are worse than either
objective applied independently.  MRL's loss requires the leading
$d$ dimensions to be informative float32 representations across five
nested truncation sizes; the straight-through binarisation gradient
simultaneously pushes the geometry toward sign-separable boundaries.
The conflict is resolved differently by different backbones: for
RoBERTa, which has more latitude to reorganise its geometry, the joint
training succeeds; for BGE and MPNet, it does not.  A sequential strategy of training MRL for float32 truncation quality and
then applying post-hoc binary quantization avoids the conflict entirely and consistently
outperforms joint training for these backbones.  This result suggests
that the design of multi-axis compression pipelines should consider
constraint compatibility explicitly rather than assuming that jointly
trained objectives reinforce one another.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Several limitations constrain the scope of conclusions that can be
drawn from this study.  First, NanoBEIR is a downsampled benchmark:
corpora of 2,000--6,000 passages are smaller than production retrieval
settings by one to two orders of magnitude, and PQ codebook quality
in particular may differ at larger scale.  Second, only three backbone
architectures are evaluated; while they span a range of retrieval
specialisation, the findings may not generalise to decoder-based or
cross-encoder models, or to backbones with non-standard embedding
dimensions.  Third, PQ codebook storage is not counted in the
per-vector memory budget, which understates the total memory cost of
PQ relative to simpler methods at small corpus scales.  Fourth, the
$\gamma$ sweep for annealed tanh covers only three values
($\{0.05, 0.10, 0.20\}$); the optimal annealing schedule for each
backbone may lie outside this range.  Finally, all evaluations are
on English-language datasets; whether the geometry hypothesis and the
backbone-conditioned method rankings extend to multilingual or
cross-lingual retrieval remains an open question.
