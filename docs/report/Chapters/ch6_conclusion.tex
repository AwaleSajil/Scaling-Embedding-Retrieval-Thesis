\chapter{Chapter 6. Conclusions}
\label{ch:conclusions}

This thesis set out to compare embedding compression methods for dense
retrieval on equal terms.  Three encoder backbones with different
retrieval priors were fine-tuned under identical conditions, evaluated
on the same benchmark (NanoBEIR), and exposed to the same family of
post-hoc, training-time, and stacked compression methods.  This chapter
revisits the research questions in
Section~\ref{sec:findings}, distils the empirical results into
operating-point guidance in Section~\ref{sec:recommendations}, and
identifies directions for future work in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

\paragraph{RQ1: Post-hoc versus training-time compression.}
Training-time compression does not yield uniformly better retrieval
quality than post-hoc compression at equivalent memory budgets.  The
answer depends on the retrieval prior of the backbone.  For BGE, which
enters compression experiments with a retrieval-aligned geometry from
large-scale contrastive pre-training, post-hoc Product Quantisation
(PQ) reaches the Pareto frontier at every compression ratio tested
and outperforms training-time binarisation at 32$\times$
(PQ-$m$96-$n$8: 0.679 vs Straight-Through Estimator binarisation (BAT):
0.658).  For RoBERTa and MPNet, which lack a retrieval prior,
training-time binarisation is firmly on the frontier at 32$\times$ and
no post-hoc method reaches it: annealed tanh ($\gamma{=}0.2$) achieves
0.580 for RoBERTa and BAT achieves 0.582 for MPNet, each substantially
above the best post-hoc configuration at the same budget
(Sections~\ref{subsec:cross_method}, \ref{subsec:ste_atanh},
\ref{sec:pareto}).

\paragraph{RQ2: Gradient surrogate and annealing schedule.}
The choice of gradient surrogate has a measurable, backbone-dependent
effect on binarisation-aware training quality.  In the standalone
setting, BAT outperforms annealed tanh for BGE and MPNet by 1.0 and 0.8
percentage points respectively, while annealed tanh outperforms BAT for
RoBERTa by 1.5 percentage points.  The annealing rate $\gamma$ for
annealed tanh shows a monotonic relationship with RoBERTa quality
across the tested range $\gamma \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.20\}$, with the
fastest schedule consistently best (Section~\ref{subsec:ste_atanh}).
A separate effect emerges in stacked training: annealed tanh is
considerably more compatible with MRL than BAT, outperforming
MRL$+$BAT on every backbone by 2.7 to 3.9 percentage points and
matching or exceeding MRL$+$post-hoc binary for RoBERTa and MPNet
(Section~\ref{sec:cross_axis}).

\paragraph{RQ3: Stacking complementary compression techniques.}
Stacking dimensional reduction (MRL truncation) with precision
reduction extends the compression frontier substantially beyond what
either method achieves alone.  For BGE, MRL$+$PQ Pareto-dominates the
standalone PQ frontier in the 48$\times$ to 96$\times$ range, and the
MRL$+$TurboQuant envelope reaches 0.614 MRR@10 at 96$\times$
compression (32 bytes per vector).  Not all stacked combinations are
better than the sum of their parts, however.  Joint MRL$+$BAT training
underperforms the sequential MRL$\rightarrow$post-hoc binary strategy
for BGE and MPNet, a reversal traced in
Section~\ref{sec:cross_axis} to gradient incompatibility between MRL's
float32 magnitude requirement and the straight-through binarisation
gradient.  The annealed tanh surrogate avoids this conflict and
recovers the joint training advantage on the general-purpose backbones.

\paragraph{RQ4: Cross-architecture generalisability.}
Findings transfer across architectures unevenly.  Up to 8$\times$
compression, method effects are backbone-agnostic in both magnitude and
direction: INT8 and TurboQuant at 4-bit precision are effectively
lossless for all three backbones, and method rank orderings are
preserved.  At 32$\times$, backbone identity becomes the dominant
factor.  Rank orderings within post-hoc methods remain stable across
backbones (PQ $>$ TurboQuant-1bit $>$ post-hoc binary), but the
ordering between training-time and post-hoc methods reverses, and the
direction of training-time effects flips: BAT reduces BGE quality below
float32 but raises RoBERTa quality above float32 by 3.5 percentage
points.  Conclusions drawn on a single retrieval-specialised backbone
therefore do not transfer to general-purpose backbones at high
compression ratios (Section~\ref{sec:cross_backbone}).

\section{Practical Recommendations}
\label{sec:recommendations}

The findings above translate into a small number of practical
recommendations, distilled from the Pareto analysis in
Section~\ref{subsec:pareto_frontiers}.

\paragraph{Up to 8$\times$.}
INT8 at 4$\times$ and TurboQuant at 4-bit (8$\times$) are
near-lossless for all three backbones and require neither retraining
nor corpus calibration beyond TurboQuant's lightweight rotation fit.
These methods are the default choice when float32 storage is no longer
acceptable but quality preservation is the dominant constraint.

\paragraph{At 32$\times$.}
Method selection becomes backbone-dependent.  For retrieval-specialised
backbones (BGE), PQ-$m$96-$n$8 is the strongest option and avoids the
cost of retraining.  For general-purpose backbones (RoBERTa, MPNet),
binarisation-aware training is necessary: annealed tanh
($\gamma{=}0.2$) for RoBERTa and BAT for MPNet.  Practitioners with
access only to a retrieval-specialised checkpoint can therefore deploy
post-hoc compression; those starting from general-purpose backbones
must invest in fine-tuning with the appropriate binarisation surrogate.

\paragraph{Beyond 32$\times$.}
Stacked combinations are the only route to the frontier.  For BGE,
MRL$+$PQ at the 48$\times$ to 96$\times$ range provides the best
quality-per-byte; MRL$+$TurboQuant extends usefully to 96$\times$ and
beyond.  For RoBERTa and MPNet, MRL$+$annealed tanh is preferred to
both MRL$+$BAT and MRL$+$post-hoc binary, providing a single
training-time recipe that combines well with MRL and degrades
gracefully as the truncation budget shrinks.

\paragraph{Surrogate gradient selection.}
When binarisation-aware training is appropriate, the surrogate
gradient should be chosen jointly with the rest of the compression
pipeline.  BAT is competitive or best in the standalone setting for
backbones with a retrieval prior.  Annealed tanh is preferred whenever
the training-time binarisation is combined with MRL, because the
gradual $\beta$ schedule avoids the gradient conflict that traps the
straight-through estimator under joint training.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

Several limitations constrain the scope of the conclusions drawn in
this thesis; each delineates a direction for future work.

\paragraph{Scale.}
NanoBEIR enables rapid comparison across a large number of compression
configurations but its corpus sizes (2{,}000 to 6{,}000 passages per
subset) are one to two orders of magnitude smaller than production
retrieval indices.  Codebook quality for PQ in particular is sensitive
to corpus scale, and the relative ranking of methods at larger scale
remains an open question.  A follow-on evaluation on full BEIR or on
production-scale corpora would establish whether the conclusions drawn
here at 56{,}000 passages transfer to indices of $10^7$ to $10^9$
passages.

\paragraph{PQ codebook overhead.}
PQ codebook storage is not counted in the per-vector memory budget
reported throughout this thesis, which understates the total memory
cost of PQ relative to simpler methods at small corpus scales. A
fuller accounting that amortises codebook size over corpus size would
sharpen the Pareto position of PQ at the lower end of the scale axis.

\paragraph{Backbone family.}
Three encoder backbones span a meaningful range of retrieval
specialisation but do not exhaust the design space.  Extension to
larger encoders (BGE-large, mxbai-embed-large), to multilingual
backbones, and to decoder-based or cross-encoder retrievers would test
whether the geometry hypothesis advanced in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} generalises beyond the BERT-family
bi-encoders studied here.

\paragraph{Multilingual and cross-lingual evaluation.}
All evaluations in this thesis are on English-language datasets.
Whether the geometry hypothesis and the backbone-conditioned method
rankings extend to multilingual or cross-lingual retrieval remains an
open question, and is a natural follow-up given the rapid adoption of
multilingual retrieval encoders in production systems.

\paragraph{Annealing schedule.}
The annealing sweep for annealed tanh covers only three values
($\gamma \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.20\}$).  The monotonic improvement
observed for RoBERTa across this range suggests that the optimum may
lie at faster schedules; a denser sweep, particularly for the backbones
that responded most strongly to atanh (RoBERTa, MPNet), would identify
the practical limit of the gradual-annealing strategy.

\paragraph{Beyond binary.}
This thesis restricts training-time precision reduction to one bit per
dimension.  Intermediate precisions (two to four bits per dimension)
trained end-to-end with annealed surrogates remain unexplored and may
offer a finer-grained quality$-$compression trade-off than either INT8
post-hoc or one-bit training-time methods provide.

\paragraph{Online and streaming corpora.}
PQ and TurboQuant codebooks are fit once on a static corpus.  In
practice, retrieval indices grow incrementally and codebook drift can
degrade quality over time.  Streaming or online variants of
corpus-fitted quantisation, particularly under the geometry conditions
that favour post-hoc methods for retrieval-specialised backbones,
would extend the practical applicability of the methods evaluated here.

\medskip
\noindent
Taken together, the results in this thesis establish that no single
compression strategy dominates across backbones or compression
budgets.  The choice of method is governed by the retrieval geometry
of the underlying encoder and by the target memory budget, and
controlled cross-backbone evaluation, of the kind conducted here, is a
prerequisite for principled method selection in dense retrieval
deployments.
