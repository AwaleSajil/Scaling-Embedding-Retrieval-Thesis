
\chapter{Chapter 1. Introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Dense vector retrieval has become a core dependency of modern AI systems. Every
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline~\cite{lewis2020retrieval}, enterprise search
engine, and question-answering service relies on the ability to find semantically relevant
content in large corpora at query time. Unlike sparse methods such as TF-IDF or BM25,
dense retrieval encodes queries and passages as continuous vector embeddings using bi-encoder
architectures~\cite{reimers2019sbert, karpukhin2020dense}, and measures semantic similarity
by cosine or dot-product distance. This enables retrieval that generalizes well beyond exact
keyword matching, which has driven rapid adoption across search, recommendation, and
generative AI applications.

As retrieval corpora scale to tens of millions or billions of passages, the memory footprint
of stored embeddings becomes a critical infrastructure bottleneck. A standard model such as
BGE-base-en-v1.5~\cite{xiao2023c} produces 768-dimensional float32 vectors, consuming
3,072~bytes per passage. Indexing one hundred million passages therefore requires
approximately 286~GB; at one billion passages this grows to approximately 2.9~TB.

Dense retrieval systems built on libraries such as FAISS~\cite{johnson2021billion} typically
require the embedding index to reside in random-access memory (RAM) for interactive query
latencies: DRAM access is on the order of 100~ns, roughly three orders of magnitude faster
than NVMe SSD random reads. Serving these in-memory indices requires memory-optimized cloud
instances; the AWS x2gd family, for example, costs approximately \$3.80 per GB per
month~\cite{aws2024pricing}. Table~\ref{tab:index-cost-intro} shows how this cost scales
with embedding dimension and corpus size across widely-used models.

As Table~\ref{tab:index-cost-intro} shows, a billion-passage index at 768 dimensions already
exceeds \$10,800 per month, and the cost more than quadruples when moving to 3,072-dimensional
models. These figures represent the minimum viable infrastructure: the memory requirement
dictates the instance size, and therefore the total cost, including compute. The problem is
growing worse as the field trends toward higher-dimensional embeddings (models such as
text-embedding-3-large at 3,072 dimensions are increasingly common) and as RAG-based
applications push corpus sizes into the billions of passages.

Beyond cost, larger embeddings also hurt speed: every query must read each document vector
from memory, so the bigger the vectors, the fewer queries the system can serve per second. For resource-constrained
environments such as on-device or edge deployments, multi-terabyte indices are simply
infeasible regardless of budget. Embedding compression is therefore not merely a storage
optimization; it is a prerequisite for deploying dense retrieval at scale.

Even simple compression approaches already show real promise: replacing each 32-bit float
with a single bit (binary quantization) shrinks the index by $32\times$ and retains much of
the retrieval quality when applied to a strong pre-trained model~\cite{shakir2024embedding}.
Compression can be pushed further by stacking multiple techniques. The open questions are how far compression can go before retrieval
quality degrades substantially, and whether incorporating compression
directly into fine-tuning (\textbf{training-time compression}) produces
better representations than compression applied to a pre-trained model
without retraining (\textbf{post-hoc compression}). This thesis
investigates both.

\section{Problem Statement}

The fundamental tension in embedding compression is between compression
depth and retrieval quality. Every compression approach trades information
for a smaller memory footprint, and the shape of this trade-off varies
across methods and compression levels.

Recent work has begun to map this trade-off systematically.
\citep{correct2025} systematically evaluate
retrieval performance across multiple post-hoc compression techniques
at various compression ratios. However, no existing study extends this
comparison to training-time compression. In most prior
work~\cite{yamada2021efficient, gan2023binary, shakir2024embedding,
qama2025}, each method is evaluated on a different base model, training
corpus, and benchmark. A method that appears to win may therefore reflect
a better starting point rather than a genuinely better compression
strategy.

A secondary gap concerns stacking. Combinations of multiple complementary
compression techniques, such as dimensionality reduction combined with
quantization, remain largely unstudied. Chapter~2 surveys the prior work
that establishes both gaps.

This thesis addresses both gaps through a controlled empirical study.
Results are reported as Pareto frontiers on the NanoBEIR
benchmark~\cite{thakur2021beir}. A Pareto frontier is the set of
configurations where no alternative achieves both higher retrieval
quality and higher compression. This directly answers the
practitioner's question: given a memory budget, which approach should
you use? The contributions are detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:contributions}.

\section{Research Questions}

This thesis is organized around four research questions:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Post-hoc versus training-time compression.}
    Does training-time compression yield better retrieval quality than
    post-hoc compression at equivalent memory budgets?

    \item \textbf{Gradient approximation strategy for binary embeddings.}
    Does the choice of gradient surrogate used during binarization training
    affect retrieval quality ?

    \item \textbf{Stacking complementary compression techniques.}
    Does combining multiple complementary compression techniques yield better
    retrieval performance to compression ratio trade-offs than either technique
    applied independently?

    \item \textbf{Cross-architecture generalizability.}
    Do the above findings hold consistently across encoder architectures
    of different retrieval starting quality, or are they model-specific?

\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions to the field of efficient dense text retrieval:

\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Systematic cross-method comparison.}
    Post-hoc and training-time compression are evaluated side by side
    under identical training data and benchmark, across three encoder
    architectures. Both single methods and stacked combinations are
    covered at multiple compression ratios. Prior work evaluates each
    method under different setups; this study provides the direct,
    fair comparison.

    \item \textbf{Binarization training strategy comparison.}
    Two gradient approximation strategies, the Straight-Through Estimator
    and Annealed Tanh, for training with a binarization objective are
    compared directly for the first time under identical
    conditions~\cite{bengio2013estimating, yamada2021efficient}.

    \item \textbf{Stacking complementary compression techniques.}
    Dimensionality reduction is systematically combined with five
    quantization techniques, covering both post-hoc and training-time
    variants. Results establish which combinations achieve the best
    retrieval quality to memory trade-offs, and whether stacking
    outperforms applying each technique independently.

    \item \textbf{Reproducible experimental pipeline.}
    We released the training codebase for training-time compression,
    supporting multi-GPU training across seven heterogeneous retrieval
    datasets with domain-weighted batch sampling. We also released the
    evaluation codebase that encodes embeddings once and applies any
    compression transform at evaluation time, avoiding redundant
    re-encoding. All results are viewable through an interactive
    dashboard with pairwise statistical significance testing across all
    model pairs.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Organization}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter 2 provides background on dense
retrieval, contrastive learning objectives, and the embedding compression methods compared in
this work. Chapter 3 describes the training and evaluation datasets, including the multi-source
training corpus construction and the NanoBEIR benchmark. Chapter 4 sets out the methodology:
the training setup and fine-tuned float32 baseline, the training-time and post-hoc compression
methods (including the Annealed Tanh Binarization approach and its relationship to the
STE-based BAT baseline), the stacked combinations, and the evaluation framework.
Chapter 5 reports the results and analysis, including per-method comparisons across three
encoder backbones, Pareto frontier analysis, and a cross-backbone generalisability study.
Chapter 6 concludes with a summary of findings, practical guidance on method selection at
given memory budgets, and directions for future work.
