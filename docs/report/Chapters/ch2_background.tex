\section{Dense Retrieval with Bi-Encoders}
\label{sec:biencoder}

\subsection{Transformer Encoders}

Modern dense retrieval systems are built on top of transformer-based language models~\cite{devlin2019bert}. A transformer encoder maps a token sequence of length $L$ to a sequence of contextualized hidden states $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_h}$, where $d_h$ is the hidden dimension, through a stack of self-attention and feed-forward layers. Each self-attention layer allows every token to attend to every other token in the sequence, producing representations that capture long-range syntactic and semantic dependencies. Bidirectional encoders such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers)~\cite{devlin2019bert} are pre-trained on large text corpora via masked language modelling (MLM), yielding general-purpose representations that transfer well to downstream tasks with minimal task-specific fine-tuning.

\subsection{Bi-Encoder Architecture}

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/biencoder_arch.pdf}
  \caption[Bi-encoder retrieval architecture.]{Bi-encoder retrieval architecture. At index time (left), all corpus passages are encoded and stored in an ANN index. At query time (right), the query is encoded and compared against the index via cosine similarity to retrieve the top-$k$ most relevant passages. Both encoders share weights.}
  \label{fig:biencoder}
\end{figure}

A bi-encoder, also called a dual encoder, consists of two transformer encoders, one for queries and one for passages, whose outputs are compressed into fixed-size dense vectors~\cite{reimers2019sbert, karpukhin2020dense}. Figure~\ref{fig:biencoder} illustrates the full bi-encoder retrieval pipeline. In practice the two towers typically share weights. Given a query $q$ and a passage $p$, each is encoded independently:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{e}_q = \text{pool}(\text{Enc}(q)), \qquad \mathbf{e}_p = \text{pool}(\text{Enc}(p)),
\end{equation}
%
where $\text{pool}(\cdot)$ is a pooling operation applied to the sequence of hidden states. Mean pooling, which averages all token hidden states, is standard for retrieval models and was established by Sentence-BERT (SBERT)~\cite{reimers2019sbert}. Relevance is scored as the cosine similarity between the query and passage embeddings:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_q, \mathbf{e}_p) = \frac{\mathbf{e}_q \cdot \mathbf{e}_p}{\|\mathbf{e}_q\| \|\mathbf{e}_p\|}.
\end{equation}
%
The architectural separation of query and passage encoding is the critical property of bi-encoders for large-scale retrieval: passage embeddings can be computed offline and stored in an approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) index, so that at query time only the query encoder runs and retrieval reduces to a single nearest-neighbor lookup~\cite{karpukhin2020dense}. This is in contrast to cross-encoders, which jointly encode the query and passage and therefore cannot pre-compute passage representations.

Exact exhaustive search, computing similarity against every passage vector, is $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot d)$ per query, where $N$ is the number of passages and $d$ is the embedding dimension. At ten million passages with 768-dimensional embeddings this requires roughly 15 billion floating-point operations per query, making sub-millisecond latency unachievable on a single machine. ANN indices such as Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW)~\cite{malkov2018efficient} and Inverted File Index (IVF)~\cite{johnson2021billion} address this by organizing vectors so that only a small fraction of the corpus is visited per query, recovering the necessary speed at a small, controlled loss in recall. Specialized disk-resident indices such as DiskANN~\cite{subramanya2019diskann} narrow this memory requirement further through SSD-optimized graph traversal, but incur latency overheads of roughly three orders of magnitude over DRAM, making in-memory indexing the standard for interactive workloads. Even with ANN indexing, the full set of passage embeddings must reside in memory for typical deployments; this memory requirement is the bottleneck this thesis addresses through embedding compression.

\textbf{BGE-base-en-v1.5}~\cite{xiao2023c} is the primary backbone. BGE (BAAI General Embedding) is a family of English bi-encoders from the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence (BAAI). The base variant was selected for its strong performance on the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB)~\cite{muennighoff2022mteb} at a moderate parameter count. Its 768-dimensional output makes the memory costs studied in this thesis directly relevant to production deployments.

\textbf{RoBERTa-base} (Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach)~\cite{liu2019roberta} is a replication study of BERT that corrects several under-training choices: it removes the Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) objective, uses dynamic masking, larger batch sizes, and 10$\times$ more training data (160\,GB versus 16\,GB). These changes yield stronger representations on standard NLU benchmarks~\cite{wang2018glue, rajpurkar2016squad} while retaining the same architecture. RoBERTa-base is included to test whether compression trade-offs observed on a retrieval-tuned model also hold for a general-purpose encoder.

\textbf{MPNet-base} (Masked and Permuted Pre-training)~\cite{song2020mpnet} combines masked language modelling with permuted language modelling. Where BERT treats other masked positions as absent when predicting a masked token, MPNet predicts tokens autoregressively in a random permutation order while attending to the full sequence, capturing dependencies between masked tokens that MLM-only models miss. MPNet-base provides a pre-training objective distinct from both BERT-style MLM and retrieval-oriented BGE, further stress-testing the generality of compression findings.

The three backbones span a range of retrieval starting quality. BGE arrives already fine-tuned for retrieval and is the strongest starting point. MPNet produces richer sentence-level representations than RoBERTa owing to its masked-and-permuted objective. RoBERTa, trained purely on token-level MLM, yields the weakest initial retrieval representations. This ordering (BGE $>$ MPNet $>$ RoBERTa) is confirmed by the NanoBEIR zero-shot baselines in Chapter~4. The central question this spread addresses is whether compression trade-offs are stable across backbones of different starting quality, or whether weaker models degrade differently under the same compression budget.

\section{Contrastive Learning for Dense Retrieval}
\label{sec:contrastive}

Contrastive learning trains a model to embed semantically related items close together and unrelated items far apart~\cite{oord2018representation}. The Information Noise-Contrastive Estimation (InfoNCE) loss formalizes this as an $N$-way classification task: given a query paired with one positive passage and $N{-}1$ negatives, the model must assign the highest similarity score to the positive pair.

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/mnrl_loss.pdf}
  \caption[Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (MNRL).]{Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss. A batch of $N$ query-passage pairs is encoded by shared-weight encoders. The $N \times N$ similarity matrix scores every query against every passage in the batch. The diagonal (green) contains positive pairs and forms the numerator of the loss; each full row forms the denominator, making each row an $N$-way softmax classification.}
  \label{fig:mnrl}
\end{figure}

The Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (MNRL) used throughout this thesis is the in-batch instantiation of InfoNCE adopted by the sentence-transformers framework~\cite{reimers2019sbert}. Negatives are obtained for free from the other examples in the same training batch: for a batch of $N$ query-positive pairs $\{(q_i, p_i^+)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, the positive passage of every other pair serves as a negative for query $q_i$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mnrl}). The loss for the full batch is:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{MNRL}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\exp\!\left(\text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_{q_i}, \mathbf{e}_{p_i^+}) / \tau\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp\!\left(\text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_{q_i}, \mathbf{e}_{p_j^+}) / \tau\right)},
  \label{eq:mnrl}
\end{equation}
%
where $\tau > 0$ is a temperature hyperparameter that controls how peaked the softmax distribution is. In-batch negatives are effective because large batch sizes expose each query to many diverse negatives simultaneously, and because batch-mates are often topically related to the query, providing challenging but informative negatives without any explicit negative mining~\cite{karpukhin2020dense}. When training data includes explicit triplets (i.e., mined hard negatives), MNRL extends naturally by appending them to the denominator alongside the in-batch negatives.

\section{Embedding Compression Methods}
\label{sec:compression_bg}

This thesis organizes embedding compression methods along two orthogonal axes.
The first axis is \textit{when} compression is applied: \textbf{post-hoc} methods
are applied to the generated embeddings without modifying the model, while \textbf{training-time}
methods incorporate compression awareness into the model's fine-tuning objective.
The second axis is \textit{how} memory is reduced: \textbf{precision reduction} decreases
the number of bits per dimension, \textbf{dimensionality reduction} decreases the number
of dimensions kept, and \textbf{learning to hash} fits a codebook that maps embeddings to
discrete codes directly, rather than applying scalar quantization per dimension.
Table~\ref{tab:compression-taxonomy} places the methods studied in this thesis along
these two axes.

STE = Straight-Through Estimator; MRL = Matryoshka Representation Learning.}
\label{tab:compression-taxonomy}

\hline

The classification in Table~\ref{tab:compression-taxonomy} reflects when
\textit{training is modified}, not when the compressed representation is
produced. Every method involves some operation at retrieval time: scalar
quantisation, sign-thresholding, table lookup, or truncation. For post-hoc
methods this retrieval-time operation is the entire compression step, applied
to a model that was not trained for it. For training-time methods the same
kind of operation is still required at retrieval (taking the sign of a BAT
embedding, truncating an MRL embedding to its first $m$ dimensions), but it
is effective only because training was modified to make that operation lossy
in a controlled way rather than destructive.

The following subsections describe each method, grouped by when compression is applied.

\subsection{Post-hoc Methods}

\subsubsection{Scalar Quantization}

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Figures/scalar_quant.pdf}
  \caption[Scalar quantization of a single embedding dimension.]{Scalar quantization of a single embedding dimension. The float32 value $x$ (left), drawn from the per-dimension calibration range $[\min_j, \max_j]$, is mapped to a signed 8-bit integer $x_q \in [-128, 127]$ (right) using 256 uniform quantization levels of width $\delta_j$. The bell curve indicates the approximate value distribution across the corpus.}
  \label{fig:scalar_quant}
\end{figure}

Scalar quantization maps each float32 dimension to a lower-precision integer, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:scalar_quant}.
For INT8 quantization, a calibration pass over a representative corpus sample
computes per-dimension bounds $[\min_j, \max_j]$, where $j$ indexes embedding
dimensions. Each value $x$ in dimension $j$ is then quantized as:
%
\begin{equation}
  x_q = \left\lfloor \frac{x - \min_j}{\delta_j} \right\rceil - 128,
  \qquad \delta_j = \frac{\max_j - \min_j}{255},
  \label{eq:int8}
\end{equation}
%
where $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ denotes rounding to the nearest integer and the
$-128$ shift maps the unsigned range $[0, 255]$ to the signed 8-bit range
$[-128, 127]$. This \textit{asymmetric} scheme uses the full 256 levels across
the observed range of each dimension, reducing storage from 4 bytes to 1 byte
per dimension ($4\times$ compression)~\cite{douze2024faiss}.

The same calibration bounds are applied to both corpus and query embeddings,
ensuring that corresponding dimensions are mapped to a consistent integer scale.

\subsubsection{Binary Quantization}

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Figures/step_function.pdf}
  \caption[Binary quantization via the sign function.]{Binary quantization maps each float32 embedding dimension $x_i$ to a binary value $b_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ via the sign function (bold line). All positive values map to $+1$ (green region) and all negative values map to $-1$ (red region). Two example dimensions illustrate the per-dimension mapping. Applied across all $d = 768$ dimensions, this achieves $32\times$ compression over float32.}
  \label{fig:binary_quant}
\end{figure}

Binary quantization replaces each float32 dimension with a single bit by applying the sign function, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:binary_quant}:
%
\begin{equation}
  b_i = \text{sign}(x_i) \in \{-1, +1\},
\end{equation}
%
where $x_i$ is the $i$-th dimension of the float32 embedding and $b_i$ is the corresponding bit, yielding $32\times$ compression over float32. Similarity between binary query vector $\mathbf{b}_q$ and passage vector $\mathbf{b}_p$ is computed via Hamming distance $d_H(\mathbf{b}_q, \mathbf{b}_p) = \sum_i \mathbf{1}[b_{q,i} \neq b_{p,i}]$, where $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function, which reduces to a bitwise XOR followed by a popcount instruction on modern hardware, enabling extremely fast similarity computation~\cite{gan2023binary}. Applied post-hoc to a float32-trained model, binary quantization is aggressive and typically incurs a non-trivial drop in retrieval quality relative to INT8.

\subsubsection{Product Quantization}

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/pq.pdf}
  \caption[Product quantization (PQ) encoding pipeline.]{Product quantization encoding pipeline. Each passage embedding $\mathbf{e}_p \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is split into $M$ equal sub-vectors. Each sub-vector is assigned to the nearest centroid in its sub-space codebook, fit offline by $k$-means on the corpus. The $M$ centroid indices form the compact PQ code, reducing storage from $d \times 4$ bytes to $M \times \lceil \log_2 K \rceil$ bits (e.g., $48\times$ compression for $M{=}64$, $K{=}256$).}
  \label{fig:pq}
\end{figure}

Product quantization (PQ)~\cite{jegou2010product} achieves high compression by dividing each $d$-dimensional embedding into $M$ equal sub-vectors and representing each sub-vector by a single integer index rather than raw floats. Figure~\ref{fig:pq} illustrates the full encoding pipeline. For each of the $M$ sub-spaces, a codebook of $K$ centroids $\{\mathbf{c}_k^{(m)}\}$ is fit by $k$-means on the corpus embeddings. Each corpus sub-vector $\mathbf{e}^{(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d/M}$ is then replaced by the index of its nearest centroid:
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{k}^{(m)} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{k} \left\|\mathbf{e}^{(m)} - \mathbf{c}_k^{(m)}\right\|.
\end{equation}
%
A passage is stored as the sequence of $M$ indices $(\hat{k}^{(1)}, \ldots, \hat{k}^{(M)})$, each requiring $\log_2 K$ bits. For $M = 64$ sub-vectors and $K = 256$ centroids, a 768-dimensional float32 embedding shrinks from 3,072 bytes to 64 bytes, a $48\times$ compression ratio.

At retrieval time, corpus embeddings are decoded from their PQ codes back to approximate float32 representations using the learned centroids, while query embeddings are kept at full float32 precision. Scoring then proceeds as standard cosine similarity between the reconstructed corpus and exact query vectors. Compressing only the passage side while keeping the query exact (asymmetric reconstruction) recovers much of the quality lost by compression~\cite{johnson2021billion}.

\subsubsection{TurboQuant}

TurboQuant~\cite{zandieh2025turboquant} is a post-hoc precision-reduction method that achieves near-optimal rate-distortion performance through three stages. First, a random orthogonal rotation (randomized Hadamard transform, $O(d \log d)$) is applied to each embedding. Rotation preserves all pairwise distances and inner products but makes each coordinate independently follow a known Beta distribution (converging to Gaussian as $d$ grows), so a single scalar quantizer works uniformly across all dimensions. Second, a Lloyd-Max quantizer (1D $k$-means on the analytic post-rotation distribution) is precomputed for each target bit-width. Lloyd-Max places non-uniform bucket boundaries that minimize mean squared error (MSE), yielding distortion within $2.7\times$ of the Shannon lower bound. Third, because Lloyd-Max reconstruction introduces bias in inner product estimation, TurboQuant allocates $b{-}1$ bits to MSE-optimal quantization and reserves 1 bit for a Quantized Johnson-Lindenstrauss (QJL) residual correction that is provably unbiased for inner products. At retrieval time, stored codes are decoded to approximate float32 vectors and scoring proceeds with standard cosine similarity.

\subsection{Training-Time Methods}

\subsubsection{Straight-Through Estimator Binarization}

Post-hoc binary quantization may be substantially improved by incorporating the sign function into the model during fine-tuning. The fundamental obstacle is that the sign function is non-differentiable at zero and has zero derivative everywhere else, making standard backpropagation impossible. The Straight-Through Estimator (STE)~\cite{bengio2013estimating} resolves this by using the sign function in the forward pass and passing gradients through it unchanged in the backward pass, as if the operation were the identity function:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{forward:} \quad \hat{\mathbf{e}} = \text{sign}(\mathbf{e}), \qquad \text{backward:} \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{e}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{e}}},
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{e}$ is the pre-binarization embedding and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}$ is its binarized counterpart. The estimator is biased (the passed gradient is not the true gradient of the sign function), yet \citeauthor{bengio2013estimating} report that it surprisingly outperforms more mathematically rigorous unbiased alternatives in practice. Training with the STE encourages the model to produce representations whose pre-binarization values are already close to $\pm 1$, so that the quantization step introduces minimal information loss at inference time. This approach is referred to in this thesis as Binarization-Aware Training (BAT) with the STE variant.

\subsubsection{Annealed Tanh Binarization}

The STE's gradient approximation is coarse: gradients are propagated regardless of the magnitude of the pre-activation, even when values are near zero and the sign function is therefore poorly approximated by the identity. Annealed Tanh binarization replaces the STE with a smooth surrogate that progressively sharpens toward the sign function over the course of training~\cite{cao2017hashnet}:
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{e}} = \tanh(\beta \cdot \mathbf{e}),
\end{equation}
%
where $\beta$ is annealed according to the schedule $\beta_t = \sqrt{\gamma \cdot t + 1}$, with $t$ the global optimizer step and $\gamma$ a growth rate hyperparameter~\cite{yamada2021efficient}. At the start of training ($t = 0$), $\beta = 1$ and $\tanh(\mathbf{e}) \approx \mathbf{e}$ for small activations, leaving the model in a nearly unconstrained float32 regime. As training progresses, $\beta$ grows and $\tanh(\beta \cdot \mathbf{e})$ sharpens toward $\text{sign}(\mathbf{e})$, gradually tightening the binarization constraint. At inference time the hard sign function is applied, recovering a standard binary representation. The annealing schedule avoids the abrupt gradient discontinuity of the STE and allows the model to adapt its representations before the constraint becomes binding. Figure~\ref{fig:atanh} shows this progression across six training checkpoints. At step~0, $\beta = 1.0$ and the surrogate reduces to $\tanh(x) \approx x$, nearly linear. At step~8{,}000, $\beta = 28.3$ and the curve is nearly indistinguishable from the hard $\text{sign}(x)$ applied at inference.

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/atanh.pdf}
  \caption[Annealed Tanh binarization surrogate at six training checkpoints.]{Annealed Tanh binarization surrogate $\tanh(\beta_t \cdot x)$ at six training checkpoints under the schedule $\beta_t = \sqrt{\gamma \cdot t + 1}$ with $\gamma = 0.1$. The surrogate sharpens from a nearly linear curve at step~0 ($\beta = 1.0$) toward the hard sign function at step~8{,}000 ($\beta = 28.3$, dashed), which is applied at inference time.}
  \label{fig:atanh}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Matryoshka Representation Learning}
\label{sec:mrl}

Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL)~\cite{kusupati2022matryoshka} trains a model to produce embeddings in which every leading $m$-dimensional prefix $\mathbf{e}_{1:m}$ is independently useful for retrieval. The core insight is that if the model is trained to solve the retrieval task at multiple granularities simultaneously, the first few dimensions will capture the most discriminative information, and later dimensions contribute increasingly fine-grained detail, analogous to the nested structure of Russian matryoshka dolls.

\begin{figure}[!h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{Figures/mrl.pdf}
  \caption[Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) training.]{MRL training. The passage embedding $\mathbf{e}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ is truncated to each prefix $\mathbf{e}_{1:m}$ for $m \in \mathcal{M} = \{32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 768\}$. A separate MNRL loss is computed for each truncation and the final loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MRL}}$ is their weighted sum (Equation~\ref{eq:mrl}).}
  \label{fig:mrl}
\end{figure}

MRL augments the standard MNRL objective with losses computed at a fixed set of nested dimensionalities $\mathcal{M} = \{m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_K\}$ where $m_1 < m_2 < \cdots < m_K = d$, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:mrl}. The multi-granularity loss is:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{MRL}} = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} c_m \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{MNRL}}\!\left(\mathbf{e}_{1:m}\right),
  \label{eq:mrl}
\end{equation}
%
where $c_m$ are positive scalar weights (set to uniform in this thesis) and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MNRL}}(\mathbf{e}_{1:m})$ is the MNRL loss computed using only the first $m$ dimensions. This thesis uses $\mathcal{M} = \{32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 768\}$ for all MRL experiments, where $768 = d$ is the full embedding dimension.

At inference time, MRL allows the embedding to be truncated to any $m \in \mathcal{M} \setminus \{d\}$ dimensions without additional fine-tuning, directly enabling post-hoc dimensionality reduction. This makes MRL embeddings qualitatively different from standard embeddings: truncating a standard embedding arbitrarily destroys retrieval quality, whereas an MRL-trained embedding is designed to remain useful at each supported granularity. The memory saving is proportional to the truncation ratio; using only the first 64 dimensions of a 768-dimensional MRL embedding yields a $12\times$ reduction.

\section{Information Retrieval Evaluation}
\label{sec:ir_eval}

\subsection{Ranking Metrics}

Retrieval systems are evaluated by how well they rank relevant documents above
irrelevant ones. All metrics in this thesis assume binary relevance: each
corpus document is either relevant or not relevant to a given query. For each
query, the retrieval system produces a ranked list of documents; metrics are
computed at cut-off $k$, meaning only the top-$k$ documents in the ranked
list are considered.

\textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR@$k$)}~\cite{voorhees1999trec} is the primary metric used in this
thesis. It measures the position of the first relevant document in the ranked
list:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{MRR}@k = \frac{1}{\text{rank}_1}, \quad
  \text{where } \text{rank}_1 = \min\{i \leq k : \text{doc}_i \in \mathcal{R}\},
  \label{eq:mrr}
\end{equation}
%
and $\text{MRR}@k = 0$ if no relevant document appears in the top $k$. MRR
is particularly sensitive to the position of the single best hit and is
well suited to retrieval tasks where there is typically one correct answer
passage per query, as is the norm across most NanoBEIR subsets.

\textbf{Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG@$k$)}~\cite{jarvelin2002cumulated} measures ranking
quality by assigning higher credit to relevant documents appearing earlier in
the list. The Discounted Cumulative Gain at position $k$ is:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\mathbf{1}[\text{doc}_i \in \mathcal{R}]}{\log_2(i+1)},
  \label{eq:dcg}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal{R}$ is the set of relevant documents and $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$
is the indicator function. NDCG@$k$ normalizes DCG@$k$ by the ideal DCG
(IDCG@$k$), the score achieved by a perfect ranking that places all relevant
documents first:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{NDCG}@k = \frac{\text{DCG}@k}{\text{IDCG}@k}, \qquad
  \text{IDCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{\min(|\mathcal{R}|,\, k)} \frac{1}{\log_2(i+1)}.
  \label{eq:ndcg}
\end{equation}
%
NDCG@$k$ lies in $[0, 1]$, with 1 indicating a perfect ranking.

\textbf{Recall@$k$} measures the fraction of all relevant documents that are
retrieved in the top $k$ results:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{Recall}@k = \frac{|\{i \leq k : \text{doc}_i \in \mathcal{R}\}|}{|\mathcal{R}|}.
  \label{eq:recall}
\end{equation}
%
Recall@$k$ is bounded above by $\min(k, |\mathcal{R}|) / |\mathcal{R}|$ and
equals 1 only when all relevant documents are retrieved within the top $k$
positions.

All three metrics are computed at $k \in \{1, 3, 5, 10\}$, averaged over all
queries in a NanoBEIR subset, and macro-averaged across the 13 subsets.

\subsection{Statistical Significance Testing}
\label{sec:significance_bg}

Retrieval benchmarks consist of a fixed set of queries, making it possible to
treat model comparisons as paired tests: each query yields one score per
model, and the question is whether one model systematically outperforms
another across the full query set.

\textbf{Shapiro--Wilk normality test}~\cite{shapiro1965analysis}. Before
choosing which significance test to interpret, the Shapiro--Wilk test checks
whether the differences $\{d_i\}$ follow a normal distribution:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0$: the differences are normally distributed.
  \item $H_1$: the differences are not normally distributed.
\end{itemize}
If $H_0$ is rejected, the normality assumption of the paired $t$-test is
violated and the Wilcoxon test is the more appropriate choice.

\textbf{Paired $t$-test}~\cite{student1908probable}. A parametric test that
assumes the differences are normally distributed and tests whether their mean
is zero:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0$: $\mu_d = 0$ (the two models have equal mean score).
  \item $H_1$: $\mu_d \neq 0$ (one model has a higher mean score).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Wilcoxon signed-rank test}~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}. A
non-parametric alternative that makes no normality assumption. For each
query, the difference $d_i$ is ranked by magnitude; the test checks whether
the positive-signed ranks (queries where model A wins) consistently outweigh
the negative-signed ranks (queries where model B wins):
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0$: the median difference is zero (neither model is better).
  \item $H_1$: the median difference is not zero.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Cohen's $d$}~\cite{cohen1988statistical} quantifies the practical
magnitude of the difference between two models:
%
\begin{equation}
  d = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d}, \qquad
  \bar{d} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i, \qquad
  s_d = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_i - \bar{d})^2}.
  \label{eq:cohens_d}
\end{equation}
%
Values of $|d| \approx 0.2$, $0.5$, and $0.8$ are conventionally interpreted
as small, medium, and large effects respectively. A statistically significant
result with a small Cohen's $d$ indicates a reliable but practically
negligible difference.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The post-hoc methods described in Section~\ref{sec:compression_bg} are already practical at scale. The Hugging Face embedding quantization survey~\cite{shakir2024embedding} showed that post-hoc binary ($32\times$) and INT8 ($4\times$) quantization retain strong MTEB quality. This established that compressed embeddings are viable for large-scale retrieval without retraining, but left open whether training-time methods can do better at the same or lower memory budget.

The STE (Section~\ref{sec:compression_bg}) was originally proposed by Bengio et al.~\cite{bengio2013estimating} for gradient estimation through stochastic neurons. Courbariaux et al.~\cite{courbariaux2016bnn} later established it as the standard for binarizing neural network weights and activations. The annealed tanh alternative originated in HashNet~\cite{cao2017hashnet} for deep hashing. Beyond binary quantization, Yang et al.~\cite{yang2019quantization} represent a $k$-level quantizer as a sum of $k{-}1$ shifted sigmoid functions with learnable biases. Shah et al.~\cite{shah2024decoupled} reduce STE bias with separate forward and backward temperatures. Schr{\"o}dter et al.~\cite{schrodter2025trainable} apply a similar sigmoid staircase to input feature compression. None of these multi-bit or decoupled variants have been applied to retrieval embeddings; they remain a natural direction for future work.

Both STE and annealed tanh have been adopted for dense retrieval. Yamada et al.~\cite{yamada2021efficient} integrated both into the Binary Passage Retriever (BPR). BPR achieves a $32\times$ memory reduction from 65~GB to 2~GB with zero accuracy loss on Natural Questions and TriviaQA. It uses binary Hamming search for candidate generation followed by float32 reranking, trained with a joint margin ranking and MNRL loss. At production scale, Gan et al.~\cite{gan2023binary} deployed recurrent residual binarization with STE at Tencent. Their approach stacks multiple binary stages to reach a user-specified bit depth, reporting 30--50\% index cost reductions across web search, video retrieval, and copyright detection. These results suggest that training-time binarization can deliver strong retrieval quality. However, each study uses a different base model and benchmark, so the effect of compression is conflated with differences in pre-training and data curation. STE and annealed tanh have never been compared directly under identical conditions; this thesis isolates that comparison.

MRL (Section~\ref{sec:mrl}) now appears prominently on the MTEB leaderboard~\cite{muennighoff2022mteb} and is attractive when memory budgets vary across deployments. SMEC~\cite{smec2025} identifies three limitations of standard MRL: gradient fluctuation from simultaneous optimization of shared encoder weights under different dimensionality losses, information degradation from blind truncation, and weak contrastive learning from easy in-batch negatives. It addresses these with sequential dimension training, adaptive dimension selection, and cross-batch memory, improving BEIR NDCG@10 by 1.9 points at 128 dimensions. QAMA~\cite{qama2025} combines MRL with multi-level quantization-aware training. It uses learnable thresholds and a hybrid precision scheme: 2-bit for the first 25\% of dimensions, 0.5-bit for the final 25\%, reaching 1.625~bits per dimension while retaining 95--98\% of full-precision NDCG@10. QAMA is the closest prior work to this thesis in method, but evaluates only on ModernBERT and MiniLM, leaving cross-architecture generality open.

CoRECT~\cite{correct2025} provides the most rigorous comparison of post-hoc methods to date. Its primary focus is how retrieval performance degrades as corpus size scales from 10K to 100M passages, evaluating eight techniques across four embedding models. It also reports performance versus compression ratio for post-hoc methods. Key findings include: quantization outperforms truncation at equal ratios, MRL training matters for truncation quality, and combining quantization with moderate truncation achieves the best recall for three of four models. CoRECT is the closest prior work in evaluation methodology, but does not fix the base model across compression types and leaves training-time methods entirely out of scope.

No existing study enables a direct comparison between post-hoc and training-time compression under controlled conditions. Every prior study evaluates a single backbone, leaving open whether compression trade-offs generalize across architectures of different retrieval starting quality. Stacked combinations involving training-time methods remain unstudied. This thesis addresses all three gaps: all compression variants share a single float32 baseline fine-tuned on the same corpus and are evaluated on NanoBEIR~\cite{nanobeir2024, thakur2021beir} across BGE-base-en-v1.5, RoBERTa-base, and MPNet-base.
