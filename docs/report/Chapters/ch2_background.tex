\section{Dense Retrieval with Bi-Encoders}
\label{sec:biencoder}

\subsection{Transformer Encoders}

Modern dense retrieval systems are built on top of transformer-based language models~\cite{devlin2019bert}. A transformer encoder maps a token sequence of length $L$ to a sequence of contextualized hidden states $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_h}$, where $d_h$ is the hidden dimension, through a stack of self-attention and feed-forward layers. Each self-attention layer allows every token to attend to every other token in the sequence, producing representations that capture long-range syntactic and semantic dependencies. Bidirectional encoders such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers)~\cite{devlin2019bert} are pre-trained on large text corpora via masked language modelling (MLM), yielding general-purpose representations that transfer well to downstream tasks with minimal task-specific fine-tuning.

\subsection{Bi-Encoder Architecture}

A bi-encoder, also called a dual encoder, consists of two transformer encoders, one for queries and one for documents, whose outputs are compressed into fixed-size dense vectors~\cite{reimers2019sbert, karpukhin2020dense}. In practice the two towers typically share weights. Given a query $q$ and a document $d$, each is encoded independently:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{e}_q = \text{pool}(\text{Enc}(q)), \qquad \mathbf{e}_d = \text{pool}(\text{Enc}(d)),
\end{equation}
%
where $\text{pool}(\cdot)$ is a pooling operation applied to the sequence of hidden states. Mean pooling, which averages all token hidden states, is standard for retrieval models and was established by Sentence-BERT (SBERT)~\cite{reimers2019sbert}. Relevance is scored as the cosine similarity between the query and document embeddings:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_q, \mathbf{e}_d) = \frac{\mathbf{e}_q \cdot \mathbf{e}_d}{\|\mathbf{e}_q\| \|\mathbf{e}_d\|}.
\end{equation}
%
The architectural separation of query and document encoding is the critical property of bi-encoders for large-scale retrieval: document embeddings can be computed offline and stored in an approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) index, so that at query time only the query encoder runs and retrieval reduces to a single nearest-neighbor lookup~\cite{karpukhin2020dense}. This is in contrast to cross-encoders, which jointly encode the query and document and therefore cannot pre-compute document representations.

Exact exhaustive search, computing similarity against every document vector, is $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot d)$ per query, where $N$ is the number of documents and $d$ is the embedding dimension. At ten million documents with 768-dimensional embeddings this requires roughly 15 billion floating-point operations per query, making sub-millisecond latency unachievable on a single machine. Approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) indices such as Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW)~\cite{malkov2018efficient} and Inverted File Index (IVF)~\cite{johnson2021billion} address this by organizing vectors so that only a small fraction of the corpus is visited per query, recovering the necessary speed at a small, controlled loss in recall. Specialized disk-resident indices such as DiskANN~\cite{subramanya2019diskann} narrow this memory requirement further through SSD-optimized graph traversal, but incur latency overheads of roughly three orders of magnitude over DRAM, making in-memory indexing the standard for interactive workloads. Even with ANN indexing, the full set of document embeddings must reside in memory for typical deployments; this memory requirement is the bottleneck this thesis addresses through embedding compression.

\subsection{BGE-base-en-v1.5}

This thesis evaluates compression methods across three backbone architectures to test whether compression trade-offs are model-specific or generalize. The primary backbone is BGE-base-en-v1.5~\cite{xiao2023c}. BGE (BAAI General Embedding) is a family of English bi-encoders released by the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence (BAAI). The \textit{base} variant uses a 12-layer BERT-like architecture with a hidden size of 768, producing 768-dimensional float32 embeddings via mean pooling. BGE-base-en-v1.5 was selected as the primary backbone because it achieves strong performance on the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB)~\cite{muennighoff2022mteb} at a moderate parameter count (109M parameters), and because its 768-dimensional output makes the memory costs and compression trade-offs studied in this thesis directly relevant to production deployments.

\subsection{RoBERTa-base}

RoBERTa (Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach)~\cite{liu2019roberta} is a replication study of BERT that identifies and corrects several under-training choices in the original recipe. Key differences from BERT include: removal of the Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) objective, dynamic masking (each training sequence receives a freshly sampled mask at each epoch rather than a static one), larger batch sizes, and substantially more training data (160\,GB versus BERT's 16\,GB). These changes together yield stronger representations than BERT on most GLUE (a suite of natural language understanding tasks spanning sentiment, inference, and similarity~\cite{wang2018glue}) and SQuAD (a reading comprehension benchmark requiring span extraction from Wikipedia passages~\cite{rajpurkar2016squad}) benchmarks while using the identical 12-layer, 768-dimensional transformer architecture (125M parameters). RoBERTa-base is included as a second backbone in this thesis to test whether the compression trade-offs observed on BGE, which is itself initialized from a retrieval-tuned BERT, also hold for a pre-training-optimized general-purpose encoder.

\subsection{MPNet-base}

MPNet (Masked and Permuted Pre-training)~\cite{song2020mpnet} addresses a dependency gap in masked language modeling: when BERT predicts a masked token it treats other masked positions as absent, so the model never explicitly learns dependencies among masked tokens. MPNet resolves this by combining masked language modeling with permuted language modeling (PLM, from XLNet): the model predicts all tokens autoregressively in a random permutation order while attending to the full original sequence, so masked-token dependencies are captured without the position-discrepancy problem of pure PLM. The result is a 12-layer, 768-dimensional encoder (110M parameters) that outperforms both BERT and RoBERTa on several sentence-level benchmarks. MPNet-base is included as a third backbone to provide a pre-training objective that is architecturally distinct from both BERT-style MLM and the retrieval-oriented BGE, further stress-testing the generality of the compression findings.

The three backbones span a range of pre-training quality for retrieval. BGE arrives already fine-tuned for retrieval and serves as the strongest starting point. MPNet produces richer sentence-level representations than RoBERTa owing to its masked-and-permuted objective, which captures dependencies between masked tokens that MLM-only models miss. RoBERTa, trained purely on token-level MLM, produces the weakest initial retrieval representations of the three. This ordering, BGE $>$ MPNet $>$ RoBERTa, is confirmed by the NanoBEIR zero-shot baseline results reported in Chapter~4. The central question this spread is designed to answer is whether compression trade-offs are stable across backbones of different starting quality, or whether a model that begins with weaker retrieval representations degrades differently under the same compression budget.

\section{Contrastive Learning for Dense Retrieval}
\label{sec:contrastive}

\subsection{The InfoNCE Objective}

Contrastive learning trains a model to embed semantically related items close together and unrelated items far apart~\cite{oord2018representation}. The Information Noise-Contrastive Estimation loss (InfoNCE) formalizes this as a $N$-way classification task: given a query $q$ paired with a positive document $p^{+}$ and $N-1$ negative documents $\{p_i^{-}\}$, the model must identify the one correct document among all candidates. The model is trained to assign the highest similarity score to the positive pair:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{InfoNCE}} = -\log \frac{\exp\!\left(\text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_q, \mathbf{e}_{p^+}) / \tau\right)}{\exp\!\left(\text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_q, \mathbf{e}_{p^+}) / \tau\right) + \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \exp\!\left(\text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_q, \mathbf{e}_{p_i^-}) / \tau\right)},
\end{equation}
%
where $\tau > 0$ is a temperature hyperparameter that controls how peaked the softmax distribution is: smaller $\tau$ sharpens the distribution toward the highest-scoring document, while larger $\tau$ flattens it across all candidates.

\subsection{Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss}

Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (MNRL) applies InfoNCE in the sentence-transformers training framework~\cite{reimers2019sbert}. In MNRL, negatives are obtained for free from the other examples in the same training batch: for a batch of $N$ query-positive pairs $\{(q_i, p_i^+)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, the positive document of every other pair in the batch serves as a negative for query $q_i$. The loss for the full batch is:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{MNRL}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\exp\!\left(\text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_{q_i}, \mathbf{e}_{p_i^+}) / \tau\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp\!\left(\text{sim}(\mathbf{e}_{q_i}, \mathbf{e}_{p_j^+}) / \tau\right)}.
  \label{eq:mnrl}
\end{equation}
%
In-batch negatives are effective because large batch sizes expose each query to many diverse negatives simultaneously, and because batch-mates are often topically related to the query, providing challenging but informative negatives without any explicit negative mining~\cite{karpukhin2020dense}. When training data includes explicit triplets (i.e., mined hard negatives), MNRL extends naturally by appending them to the denominator alongside the in-batch negatives.

\section{Embedding Compression Methods}
\label{sec:compression_bg}

Chapter~1 introduced a taxonomy of embedding compression methods along two axes: \textit{when} compression is applied (post-hoc vs.\ training-time) and \textit{how} memory is reduced (precision reduction, dimensionality reduction, or learning to hash). This section provides technical depth for each method.

\subsection{Scalar Quantization}

Scalar quantization maps each float32 dimension to a lower-precision integer.
For INT8 quantization, a calibration pass over a representative corpus sample
computes per-dimension bounds $[\min_j, \max_j]$, where $j$ indexes embedding
dimensions. Each value $x$ in dimension $j$ is then quantized as:
%
\begin{equation}
  x_q = \left\lfloor \frac{x - \min_j}{\delta_j} \right\rceil - 128,
  \qquad \delta_j = \frac{\max_j - \min_j}{255},
  \label{eq:int8}
\end{equation}
%
where $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ denotes rounding to the nearest integer and the
$-128$ shift maps the unsigned range $[0, 255]$ to the signed 8-bit range
$[-128, 127]$. This \textit{asymmetric} scheme uses the full 256 levels across
the observed range of each dimension, reducing storage from 4 bytes to 1 byte
per dimension ($4\times$ compression)~\cite{douze2024faiss}.

The same calibration bounds are applied to both corpus and query embeddings,
ensuring that corresponding dimensions are mapped to a consistent integer scale.

\subsection{Binary Quantization}

Binary quantization replaces each float32 dimension with a single bit by applying the sign function:
%
\begin{equation}
  b_i = \text{sign}(x_i) \in \{-1, +1\},
\end{equation}
%
where $x_i$ is the $i$-th dimension of the float32 embedding and $b_i$ is the corresponding bit, yielding $32\times$ compression over float32. Similarity between binary query vector $\mathbf{b}_q$ and document vector $\mathbf{b}_d$ is computed via Hamming distance $d_H(\mathbf{b}_q, \mathbf{b}_d) = \sum_i \mathbf{1}[b_{q,i} \neq b_{d,i}]$, where $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function, which reduces to a bitwise XOR followed by a popcount instruction on modern hardware, enabling extremely fast similarity computation~\cite{gan2023binary}. Applied post-hoc to a float32-trained model, binary quantization is aggressive and typically incurs a non-trivial drop in retrieval quality relative to INT8.
\subsection{Product Quantization}

Product quantization (PQ)~\cite{jegou2010product} achieves high compression by dividing each $d$-dimensional embedding into $M$ equal sub-vectors and representing each sub-vector by a single integer index rather than raw floats. For each of the $M$ sub-spaces, a codebook of $K$ centroids $\{\mathbf{c}_k^{(m)}\}$ is fit by $k$-means on the corpus embeddings. Each corpus sub-vector $\mathbf{e}^{(m)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d/M}$ is then replaced by the index of its nearest centroid:
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{k}^{(m)} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{k} \left\|\mathbf{e}^{(m)} - \mathbf{c}_k^{(m)}\right\|.
\end{equation}
%
A document is stored as the sequence of $M$ indices $(\hat{k}^{(1)}, \ldots, \hat{k}^{(M)})$, each requiring $\log_2 K$ bits. For $M = 64$ sub-vectors and $K = 256$ centroids, a 768-dimensional float32 embedding shrinks from 3,072 bytes to 64 bytes, a $48\times$ compression ratio.

At retrieval time, corpus embeddings are decoded from their PQ codes back to approximate float32 representations using the learned centroids, while query embeddings are kept at full float32 precision. Scoring then proceeds as standard cosine similarity between the reconstructed corpus and exact query vectors. Compressing only the document side while keeping the query exact (asymmetric reconstruction) recovers much of the quality lost by compression~\cite{johnson2021billion}.

\subsection{TurboQuant}

TurboQuant~\cite{zandieh2025turboquant} is a \textit{post-hoc, precision-reduction} method: it is calibrated on corpus embeddings after the embedding model has run, with no retraining of the model itself. It claims near-optimal rate-distortion performance: for a given number of bits, it loses less retrieval-relevant information than scalar or product quantization methods can achieve. This is possible because TurboQuant fits a global non-linear quantizer that minimises reconstruction error across the full embedding dimension jointly, rather than partitioning dimensions into independent sub-codebooks. Originally developed for online quantization of key-value (KV) caches in large language model inference, its structure applies directly to offline retrieval index compression. At retrieval time, stored codes are decoded back to approximate float32 vectors, and scoring proceeds with standard cosine similarity.

\subsection{Straight-Through Estimator Binarization}

Post-hoc binary quantization may be substantially improved by incorporating the sign function into the model architecture during fine-tuning, a hypothesis this thesis evaluates empirically. The fundamental obstacle is that the sign function is non-differentiable at zero and has zero derivative everywhere else, making standard backpropagation impossible. The Straight-Through Estimator (STE)~\cite{bengio2013estimating} resolves this by using the sign function in the forward pass and passing gradients through it unchanged in the backward pass, as if the operation were the identity function:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{forward:} \quad \hat{\mathbf{e}} = \text{sign}(\mathbf{e}), \qquad \text{backward:} \quad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{e}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{e}}},
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{e}$ is the pre-binarization embedding and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}$ is its binarized counterpart. The estimator is biased (the passed gradient is not the true gradient of the sign function), yet \citeauthor{bengio2013estimating} report that it surprisingly outperforms more mathematically rigorous unbiased alternatives in practice. Training with the STE encourages the model to produce representations whose pre-binarization values are already close to $\pm 1$, so that the quantization step introduces minimal information loss at inference time. This approach is referred to in this thesis as Binarization-Aware Training (BAT) with the STE variant.

\subsection{Annealed Tanh Binarization}

The STE's gradient approximation is coarse: gradients are propagated regardless of the magnitude of the pre-activation, even when values are near zero and the sign function is therefore poorly approximated by the identity. Annealed Tanh binarization replaces the STE with a smooth surrogate that progressively sharpens toward the sign function over the course of training~\cite{cao2017hashnet}:
%
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{e}} = \tanh(\beta \cdot \mathbf{e}),
\end{equation}
%
where $\beta$ is annealed according to the schedule $\beta_t = \sqrt{\gamma \cdot t + 1}$, with $t$ the global optimizer step and $\gamma$ a growth rate hyperparameter~\cite{yamada2021efficient}. At the start of training ($t = 0$), $\beta = 1$ and $\tanh(\mathbf{e}) \approx \mathbf{e}$ for small activations, leaving the model in a nearly unconstrained float32 regime. As training progresses, $\beta$ grows and $\tanh(\beta \cdot \mathbf{e})$ sharpens toward $\text{sign}(\mathbf{e})$, gradually tightening the binarization constraint. At inference time the hard sign function is applied, recovering a standard binary representation. The annealing schedule avoids the abrupt gradient discontinuity of the STE and allows the model to adapt its representations before the constraint becomes binding.

\subsection{Matryoshka Representation Learning}
\label{sec:mrl}

Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL)~\cite{kusupati2022matryoshka} trains a model to produce embeddings in which every leading $m$-dimensional prefix $\mathbf{e}_{1:m}$ is independently useful for retrieval. The core insight is that if the model is trained to solve the retrieval task at multiple granularities simultaneously, the first few dimensions will capture the most discriminative information, and later dimensions contribute increasingly fine-grained detail, analogous to the nested structure of Russian matryoshka dolls.

MRL augments the standard MNRL objective with losses computed at a fixed set of nested dimensionalities $\mathcal{M} = \{m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_K\}$ where $m_1 < m_2 < \cdots < m_K = d$. The multi-granularity loss is:
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{MRL}} = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} c_m \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{MNRL}}\!\left(\mathbf{e}_{1:m}\right),
  \label{eq:mrl}
\end{equation}
%
where $c_m$ are positive scalar weights (set to uniform in this thesis) and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MNRL}}(\mathbf{e}_{1:m})$ is the MNRL loss computed using only the first $m$ dimensions. This thesis uses $\mathcal{M} = \{32, 64, 128, 256, 512\}$ for all MRL experiments.

At inference time, MRL allows the embedding to be truncated to any $m \in \mathcal{M}$ dimensions without additional fine-tuning, directly enabling post-hoc dimensionality reduction. This makes MRL embeddings qualitatively different from standard embeddings: truncating a standard embedding arbitrarily destroys retrieval quality, whereas an MRL-trained embedding is designed to remain useful at each supported granularity. The memory saving is proportional to the truncation ratio; using only the first 64 dimensions of a 512-dimensional MRL embedding yields an $8\times$ reduction.

\section{Information Retrieval Evaluation}
\label{sec:ir_eval}

\subsection{Ranking Metrics}

Retrieval systems are evaluated by how well they rank relevant documents above
irrelevant ones. All metrics in this thesis assume binary relevance: each
corpus document is either relevant or not relevant to a given query. For each
query, the retrieval system produces a ranked list of documents; metrics are
computed at cut-off $k$, meaning only the top-$k$ documents in the ranked
list are considered.

\textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR@$k$)}~\cite{voorhees1999trec} is the primary metric used in this
thesis. It measures the position of the first relevant document in the ranked
list:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{MRR}@k = \frac{1}{\text{rank}_1}, \quad
  \text{where } \text{rank}_1 = \min\{i \leq k : \text{doc}_i \in \mathcal{R}\},
  \label{eq:mrr}
\end{equation}
%
and $\text{MRR}@k = 0$ if no relevant document appears in the top $k$. MRR
is particularly sensitive to the position of the single best hit and is
well suited to retrieval tasks where there is typically one correct answer
passage per query, as is the norm across most NanoBEIR subsets.

\textbf{Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG@$k$)}~\cite{jarvelin2002cumulated} measures ranking
quality by assigning higher credit to relevant documents appearing earlier in
the list. The Discounted Cumulative Gain at position $k$ is:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\mathbf{1}[\text{doc}_i \in \mathcal{R}]}{\log_2(i+1)},
  \label{eq:dcg}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal{R}$ is the set of relevant documents and $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$
is the indicator function. NDCG@$k$ normalizes DCG@$k$ by the ideal DCG
(IDCG@$k$), the score achieved by a perfect ranking that places all relevant
documents first:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{NDCG}@k = \frac{\text{DCG}@k}{\text{IDCG}@k}, \qquad
  \text{IDCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{\min(|\mathcal{R}|,\, k)} \frac{1}{\log_2(i+1)}.
  \label{eq:ndcg}
\end{equation}
%
NDCG@$k$ lies in $[0, 1]$, with 1 indicating a perfect ranking.

\textbf{Recall@$k$} measures the fraction of all relevant documents that are
retrieved in the top $k$ results:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{Recall}@k = \frac{|\{i \leq k : \text{doc}_i \in \mathcal{R}\}|}{|\mathcal{R}|}.
  \label{eq:recall}
\end{equation}
%
Recall@$k$ is bounded above by $\min(k, |\mathcal{R}|) / |\mathcal{R}|$ and
equals 1 only when all relevant documents are retrieved within the top $k$
positions.

\textbf{Accuracy@$k$} (also called hit rate) is a binary metric indicating
whether at least one relevant document appears in the top $k$ results:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{Accuracy}@k = \mathbf{1}\!\left[\exists\, i \leq k : \text{doc}_i \in \mathcal{R}\right].
  \label{eq:acc}
\end{equation}

All four metrics are computed at $k \in \{1, 3, 5, 10\}$, averaged over all
queries in a NanoBEIR subset, and macro-averaged across the 13 subsets.

\subsection{Statistical Significance Testing}
\label{sec:significance_bg}

Retrieval benchmarks consist of a fixed set of queries, making it possible to
treat model comparisons as paired tests: each query yields one score per
model, and the question is whether one model systematically outperforms
another across the full query set.

\textbf{Shapiro--Wilk normality test}~\cite{shapiro1965analysis}. Before
choosing which significance test to interpret, the Shapiro--Wilk test checks
whether the differences $\{d_i\}$ follow a normal distribution:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0$: the differences are normally distributed.
  \item $H_1$: the differences are not normally distributed.
\end{itemize}
If $H_0$ is rejected, the normality assumption of the paired $t$-test is
violated and the Wilcoxon test is the more appropriate choice.

\textbf{Paired $t$-test}~\cite{student1908probable}. A parametric test that
assumes the differences are normally distributed and tests whether their mean
is zero:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0$: $\mu_d = 0$ (the two models have equal mean score).
  \item $H_1$: $\mu_d \neq 0$ (one model has a higher mean score).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Wilcoxon signed-rank test}~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}. A
non-parametric alternative that makes no normality assumption. For each
query, the difference $d_i$ is ranked by magnitude; the test checks whether
the positive-signed ranks (queries where model A wins) consistently outweigh
the negative-signed ranks (queries where model B wins):
\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_0$: the median difference is zero (neither model is better).
  \item $H_1$: the median difference is not zero.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Cohen's $d$}~\cite{cohen1988statistical} measures the practical
magnitude of the difference between two models. It is computed as the mean of
the per-query differences divided by their standard deviation:
%
\begin{equation}
  d = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d}, \qquad
  \bar{d} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i, \qquad
  s_d = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(d_i - \bar{d})^2}.
  \label{eq:cohens_d}
\end{equation}
%
Cohen's $d$ quantifies the practical magnitude of the difference: values of
$|d| \approx 0.2$, $0.5$, and $0.8$ are conventionally interpreted as small,
medium, and large effects respectively. A statistically significant result
with a small Cohen's $d$ indicates a reliable but practically negligible
difference.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Early evidence that discrete embeddings can match float32 retrieval quality came from
Yamada et al.~\cite{yamada2021efficient}, whose Binary Passage Retriever (BPR) integrated
learning-to-hash into the dense passage retrieval framework, achieving a $32\times$ memory
reduction from 65~GB to 2~GB with zero accuracy loss on Natural Questions and TriviaQA.
BPR uses a two-stage pipeline: binary Hamming search over the full corpus for candidate
generation, followed by float32 inner product reranking over the shortlist. Training
combines a margin ranking loss on the binary codes with MNRL on the float32 embeddings
jointly. At production scale, Gan et al.~\cite{gan2023binary} deployed binary retrieval at
Tencent using recurrent residual binarization with STE for gradient flow, reporting
30--50\% index cost reductions across web search, video retrieval, and copyright detection.
The Hugging Face embedding quantization survey~\cite{shakir2024embedding} established that
even post-hoc binary and INT8 quantization, applied without retraining, retains strong MTEB
quality when paired with asymmetric scoring. These results collectively establish that
compressed embeddings are viable for large-scale retrieval. Each study evaluates a different
base model on a different benchmark, however, so cross-method comparisons are unreliable:
the effect of compression is conflated with differences in pre-training, fine-tuning, and
data curation.

The key technical challenge in training-time compression is that quantization functions are
non-differentiable. Two families of solutions have been established. The
STE~\cite{bengio2013estimating} passes gradients through the sign function unchanged.
Courbariaux et al.~\cite{courbariaux2016bnn} established it as the standard approach for
binarizing neural network weights and activations; both BPR~\cite{yamada2021efficient} and
Gan et al.~\cite{gan2023binary} rely on it for retrieval binarization. The alternative is
the continuation approach of Cao et al.~\cite{cao2017hashnet}, which replaces the sign
function with tanh activations whose temperature is progressively annealed during training
until each tanh approximates a step function. BPR adapts this schedule to passage retrieval
alongside STE. The annealed tanh experiments in this thesis build on the same lineage,
applying temperature annealing within the sentence-transformers framework with MNRL as the
sole training objective and no float32 reranking stage. STE and annealed tanh have each
been used for retrieval binarization in prior work, but have never been compared directly
under identical conditions; this thesis isolates that comparison. Beyond these binary
approaches, Shah et al.~\cite{shah2024decoupled} propose a Decoupled STE that uses
separate temperatures for the forward and backward passes, reducing the bias inherent in
the standard estimator. For multi-bit quantization, Yang et al.~\cite{yang2019quantization}
represent a $k$-level quantizer as a sum of $k{-}1$ shifted sigmoid functions with
learnable biases, each sharpened by an annealed temperature. Schr{\"o}dter
et al.~\cite{schrodter2025trainable} apply a similar sigmoid staircase to input feature
compression, achieving 5--16$\times$ compression with modest accuracy loss. Neither
approach has been applied to retrieval embeddings; both are directions for future work.

Kusupati et al.~\cite{kusupati2022matryoshka} introduced MRL, training a single embedding
so that any prefix of dimensions forms a useful representation. MRL now appears prominently
on the MTEB leaderboard~\cite{muennighoff2022mteb} and offers an attractive operating point
when memory and latency budgets vary by deployment context. Sequential Matryoshka Embedding
Compression (SMEC)~\cite{smec2025} identifies three limitations of standard MRL: gradient
fluctuation from jointly optimizing all dimension scales simultaneously, information
degradation from blind truncation, and weak contrastive learning from easy in-batch
negatives. Addressing these with sequential dimension training, adaptive dimension
selection, and cross-batch memory, SMEC improves BEIR NDCG@10 by 1.9 points at 128
dimensions over MRL baselines on LLM-scale models. Quantization-Aware Matryoshka
Adaptation (QAMA)~\cite{qama2025}, the closest prior work to this thesis, combines MRL
with multi-level quantization-aware training. QAMA uses learnable thresholds initialized
from percentile statistics and updated by exponential moving average, and applies a hybrid
precision scheme that allocates higher bits to early MRL dimensions (2-bit for the first
25\%) and progressively lower bits to later ones (0.5-bit for the final 25\%), reaching an
average of 1.625~bits per dimension while retaining 95--98\% of full-precision NDCG@10.
QAMA evaluates on ModernBERT and MiniLM, leaving open whether these findings generalize
across architectures with different retrieval starting quality.

Controlled Retrieval Evaluation of Compression Techniques
(CoRECT)~\cite{correct2025} provides the most rigorous comparison of post-hoc methods to
date, evaluating eight compression techniques across four embedding models on a benchmark
spanning 10K to 100M passages. Its findings are directly relevant to this thesis:
quantization outperforms dimensionality truncation at equal compression ratios, MRL
training matters significantly for truncation quality, and combining quantization with
moderate truncation achieves the best recall for three of the four models tested. CoRECT
does not fix the base model across compression types, however, and leaves training-time
methods entirely out of scope. No existing study therefore enables a direct comparison
between post-hoc and training-time compression under controlled conditions. Every prior
study also evaluates a single backbone, leaving open whether compression trade-offs are
model-specific or generalize across architectures with different retrieval starting quality.
Stacked combinations involving training-time methods, such as MRL jointly trained with
binarization, remain unstudied. This thesis addresses all three gaps: all compression
variants share a single float32 baseline fine-tuned on the same corpus and are evaluated on
NanoBEIR~\cite{nanobeir2024, thakur2021beir} across BGE-base-en-v1.5, RoBERTa-base, and
MPNet-base.
